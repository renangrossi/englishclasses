A2 Review: Small Words That Carry a Lot

Prepositions, quantifiers and comparatives — the parts of English that look easy and are not.

The hardest words in English are not the long ones. They are the short ones, and there are three groups of them that give trouble at this level.

First, prepositions of place. The rough rule is that IN is for something enclosed — in a bag, in a room, in New York — ON is for a surface — on the table, on the wall, on the floor — and AT is for a point rather than a space: at the door, at the airport, at the bus stop. The same three words do time as well, and the pattern is similar: IN a month or a year, ON a day, AT a clock time. In June, on Monday, at eight o'clock. There is no logic to learn beyond that, and the only way to fix it is to meet the phrases often enough that the wrong one starts to sound wrong.

A few more are worth having: THROUGH for going in one side and out the other, BETWEEN for two things, AMONG for many, INTO for movement to the inside, and UNDER for underneath.

Second, the quantifiers. English divides nouns into two kinds and treats them differently, which most languages do not. You can count chairs, eggs and questions, so they take MANY and A FEW. You cannot count milk, money, time or information, so they take MUCH and A LITTLE. “How many eggs?” but “How much milk?” Get this wrong and everybody still understands you, which is exactly why it is so easy to keep getting it wrong for years.

There is also a small difference that matters more than its size suggests. A FEW and A LITTLE are positive: I have a few friends here means you are not alone. FEW and LITTLE without the article are negative: I have few friends here means you are lonely. One small word changes the meaning of the sentence completely.

SOME, ANY and NO follow their own pattern. SOME goes in positive sentences — I have some questions — and also in offers and requests, which is the exception: Would you like some coffee? ANY goes in negatives and questions: I do not have any questions. Is there any milk? NO replaces “not any”: There is no milk, which means the same as there is not any milk but is shorter and stronger.

Third, comparatives. Short words add -ER and -EST: cheap, cheaper, the cheapest. Long words take MORE and THE MOST: expensive, more expensive, the most expensive. The dividing line is roughly two syllables, and the usual mistake is to do both at once — “more cheaper” is the single most common error at this level. And the irregular ones have to be learned: good, better, the best; bad, worse, the worst; far, further, the furthest.

Vocabulary

  • enclosed — closed in on all sides
  • surface — the flat outside part of something
  • underneath — below and covered by something
  • quantifier — a word showing how much or how many, such as some or many
  • to count — to say how many there are
  • lonely — unhappy because you are alone
  • offer — when you ask somebody if they would like something
  • request — when you ask for something politely
  • to replace — to be used instead of something else
  • syllable — a single beat in a spoken word
  • dividing line — the point where one group ends and another begins
  • to sound wrong — to feel incorrect to a listener, before you can explain why

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Describe your room using six prepositions of place.
  • “I have a few friends here” and “I have few friends here” mean different things. Does your own language make that distinction?
  • Which countable/uncountable pairs catch you out? (Information, advice, news and furniture are the usual ones.)
  • Compare three places you have lived or visited, using a comparative and a superlative.
  • Write about 100 words comparing two cities, using at least three comparatives and one superlative.
