Data Centers and Digital Agents

The buildings that make modern AI possible, and the shift from software that answers to software that acts.

The Text

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Hover over a highlighted word to see what it means. 3:54

Two developments have done more than any others to change what software can do: an enormous increase in the computing power available to train artificial intelligence, and a change in what that software is asked to do once trained. The first is a story about buildings. The second is a story about responsibility.

Modern AI systems — particularly large language models and the multimodal systems that handle text, images and audio together — require computing power on a scale that has no real precedent in civilian technology. That power lives in hyperscale data centers: purpose-built facilities, often the size of several warehouses, packed with specialized processors and built for nothing except training and running these models.

The engineering problem such buildings present is less about computing than about heat and electricity. Packing tens of thousands of processors into one facility produces an extraordinary thermal load, and the power draw of a large site can approach that of a small city. Operators have responded by rethinking cooling — liquid systems in place of moving cold air around — and by siting facilities where power is abundant or where they can be built alongside dedicated generation. Whether the resulting energy demand can be met without a significant increase in emissions is a genuinely open question, and one that the industry is under growing pressure to answer rather than defer.

The reason for all this construction is that the capability of these systems has, so far, scaled with the resources devoted to them. More computing power and more data have generally produced more capable models. Whether that relationship continues indefinitely is disputed, but it has held long enough to justify very large amounts of capital, and it explains why organizations that cannot build such facilities increasingly rent time on someone else's.

The second development matters more for how the software is actually used. Earlier systems were generative: you supplied a prompt and they produced text, an image or code in response. Each exchange stood on its own, and the person remained in control of every step. Newer systems are described as agentic. Given an objective rather than an instruction, they break it into steps, choose and use tools, evaluate their own intermediate results, recover from some of their own errors, and work at a task over hours or days with limited supervision.

The practical difference is substantial. An agentic system can work inside a real codebase, make design decisions, run searches on its own initiative and act on what it finds. Organizations are already deploying such systems in software development, customer operations and research support, and the direction of travel is clear enough that the interesting questions have moved on from whether the technology works.

Those questions are mostly about oversight. A tool that answers a question can be checked before anything happens. A system that acts over an extended period makes many small decisions that nobody reviews individually, and errors can compound before they are noticed. The organizations getting the most out of these systems tend to be the ones that have thought hardest about where a human being is required to approve something, what the software is permitted to touch, and how its work is logged so that a mistake can be traced afterwards. Enthusiasm is easy to find; the constraints are what distinguish a serious deployment from an experiment.

It is worth being careful about the analogies. Calling these systems colleagues or interns flatters them: they do not understand the organization they work in, they have no stake in the outcome, and their confidence is unrelated to their accuracy. What is genuinely new is narrower and still significant — software that can be given a goal instead of an instruction, and that will do a useful amount of the intervening work without being told how.

Vocabulary

  • multimodal — handling more than one kind of input, such as text and images together
  • precedent — an earlier case that serves as a comparison
  • hyperscale — built for computing at the very largest scale
  • purpose-built — designed and constructed for one specific use
  • thermal load — the total amount of heat that has to be removed from a space
  • power draw — the quantity of electricity a system consumes while running
  • to site — to choose a location for something and build it there
  • abundant — available in large quantities
  • emissions — the greenhouse gases released by an activity
  • to defer — to put off dealing with something
  • to scale with — to increase in proportion to something else
  • disputed — argued about; not settled
  • capital — money invested in building something that will produce returns
  • generative — producing new content in response to a prompt
  • prompt — the instruction or question given to an AI system
  • agentic — able to pursue a goal independently rather than responding to single instructions
  • intermediate — occurring between the start and the end of a process
  • supervision — the act of watching over work as it is done
  • codebase — the whole body of source code belonging to a project
  • oversight — responsible supervision of a process, to catch what goes wrong
  • to compound — to accumulate, each error making the next one worse
  • to log — to record actions as they happen so they can be reviewed later
  • to flatter — to describe something as better than it is
  • stake — a personal interest in how something turns out

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The writer says enthusiasm is easy and constraints are what matter. What constraints would you want before letting software act on your behalf?
  • Is it fair to describe an AI system as a colleague? What does the metaphor get right, and what does it hide?
  • Who should bear the environmental cost of computing at this scale — the operators, the customers, or the users?
  • “Their confidence is unrelated to their accuracy.” Why does that combination make a system harder to supervise than one that is simply unreliable?
  • Write about 150 words describing a task you would be willing to hand to an autonomous system, and one you would not, with your reasons.
