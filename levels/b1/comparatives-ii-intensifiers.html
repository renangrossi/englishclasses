B1

Comparatives II — Intensifiers & Double Comparatives

Make comparisons stronger, softer, or show two things changing together.

ObjectivesRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummary

Introduction

You already know how to form comparatives (taller, more interesting). Now let's control exactly how big the difference is, and how to link two comparisons together.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Strengthen a comparison with much, far, a lot, way
  • Soften a comparison with slightly, a bit, a little
  • Use double comparatives to show two things increasing or decreasing together
  • Use as...as and nowhere near as...as for comparisons of equality and clear inequality

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Strengthening a comparative

much / far / a lot / way (informal) + comparative — for a big difference.

  • This phone is much more expensive than that one.
  • She's far more experienced than her colleagues.
  • Today is way colder than yesterday. (informal)

b) Softening a comparative

slightly / a bit / a little + comparative — for a small difference.

  • This route is slightly longer, but it's more scenic.
  • I'm feeling a little better today.

c) Double comparatives

the + comparative..., the + comparative... — shows two things changing together.

  • The more you practice, the better you get.
  • The sooner we leave, the sooner we'll arrive.
  • The older I get, the less I worry about small things.

d) as...as and nowhere near as...as

as + adjective + as for equality; not as...as for inequality; nowhere near as...as for a big, clear difference.

  • This test was as difficult as the last one. (equal)
  • This test wasn't as difficult as the last one. (less difficult)
  • This test was nowhere near as difficult as the last one. (much less difficult — a strong contrast)

Examples

See It in Action

  • The new stadium is much bigger than the old one.
  • She sings far better than I do.
  • This coffee is slightly stronger than usual.
  • He's a bit taller than his brother.
  • The more you read, the more you learn.
  • The harder you work, the luckier you get.
  • This city isn't as expensive as London.
  • The results were nowhere near as good as we expected.
  • This laptop is way faster than my old one.
  • The earlier you book, the cheaper the tickets are.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidThis phone is very more expensive.

Use insteadThis phone is much more expensive. / This phone is a lot more expensive.

"Very" doesn't intensify comparatives — use much/far/a lot instead.

AvoidThe more you practice, better you get.

Use insteadThe more you practice, the better you get.

Both halves of a double comparative need "the" before the comparative form.

AvoidThis city is more expensive as London.

Use insteadThis city is as expensive as London. / This city is more expensive than London.

Don't mix the two patterns — "as...as" for equality, "more...than" for a one-way comparison, never "more...as."

AvoidThe exam was slightly more difficult a lot.

Use insteadThe exam was slightly more difficult.

Use only one intensifier at a time — don't combine a softener (slightly) with a strengthener (a lot).

AvoidIt's nowhere as difficult than the last one.

Use insteadIt's nowhere near as difficult as the last one.

The full expression is "nowhere near as...as," not "nowhere as...than."

Summary

Review

  • Strengthen a comparative with much/far/a lot, never “very.”
  • Soften a comparative with slightly/a bit/a little.
  • Double comparatives (the + comparative, the + comparative) show two things changing together.
  • As...as = equal; not as...as = less; nowhere near as...as = a big, clear difference.
