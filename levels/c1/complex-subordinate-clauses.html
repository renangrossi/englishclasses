C1

Complex Subordinate Clauses

Link ideas with precision using concessive, purpose and result clauses.

ObjectivesExplanationRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummaryTest Yourself

Introduction

Subordinate clauses let you link a main idea to a secondary one with precision. At C1 you need to control three families beyond simple “because” / “although”.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Use concessive clauses (even though, whereas, despite/in spite of) to show contrast
  • Use purpose clauses (so as to, in order to/that) to explain why something is done
  • Use result clauses (so...that, such...that) to show the consequence of a degree or quality
  • Choose the right register for each connector in formal writing versus speech

Explanation

Explanation

Register

“In order that,” “so as to,” and “whereas” belong to formal/written English; “so,” “although,” and “but” are fine in speech.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Concessive clauses (contrast)

even though, although, though, whereas, while, in spite of / despite (+ noun or -ing)

  • “Even though / although / though” + clause show a surprising contrast: Even though it was raining, we went for a walk.
  • “Whereas / while” contrast two facts neutrally, often side by side: She loves cities, whereas he prefers the countryside.
  • “In spite of / despite” + noun/gerund (never a full clause directly): Despite the rain, we went for a walk. / Despite working hard, she failed.

b) Purpose clauses

so as to, in order to/that, for the purpose of

  • “So as to” / “in order to” + bare infinitive express purpose formally: We left early so as to avoid the traffic.
  • “In order that” / “so that” + clause (with can/could/will/would) are used when the subject changes: I gave her my number so that she could contact me.

c) Result clauses

such...that, so...that

  • “So” + adjective/adverb + “that”: The exam was so difficult that half the class failed.
  • “Such” + (a/an) + adjective + noun + “that”: It was such a difficult exam that half the class failed.

Examples

See It in Action

  • Even though she was exhausted, she finished the marathon.
  • Whereas the north of the country is mountainous, the south is flat.
  • Despite having no experience, he got the job.
  • In spite of the criticism, the director refused to change the ending.
  • We arrived early so as to get good seats.
  • She whispered so that no one else would hear.
  • He studied hard in order to pass the bar exam.
  • The music was so loud that we couldn't hear each other talk.
  • It was such a compelling argument that the jury changed its mind.
  • While I understand your concerns, I still disagree with the decision.
  • The report was so poorly written that it had to be redone.
  • He left the company in order that his rival could take over.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidDespite he was tired, he kept working.

Use insteadDespite being tired, he kept working. / Although he was tired, he kept working.

“Despite/in spite of” take a noun or gerund, not a subject + verb clause.

AvoidThe film was such interesting that I watched it twice.

Use insteadThe film was so interesting that I watched it twice.

“So” goes with an adjective alone; “such” needs a noun (such an interesting film).

AvoidShe works hard so as she can support her family.

Use insteadShe works hard so that she can support her family. / She works hard in order to support her family.

“So as to” takes an infinitive, not a clause; use “so that” for a full clause with a modal.

AvoidWhereas he is generous, but she is quite mean.

Use insteadWhereas he is generous, she is quite mean.

Don't combine “whereas” with “but” — the contrast is already built into “whereas.”

AvoidIt was so a boring lecture that half the room fell asleep.

Use insteadIt was such a boring lecture that half the room fell asleep.

With “adjective + noun,” use “such (a/an),” not “so.”

Summary

Review

  • Concessive clauses (even though, whereas, despite/in spite of) show contrast — despite/in spite of take a noun or -ing, never a full clause.
  • Purpose clauses (so as to, in order to/that) explain why — so as to/in order to take an infinitive; so that/in order that take a clause with a modal.
  • Result clauses use so + adjective + that, or such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + that.
  • In order that, so as to and whereas are formal/written; so, although and but are fine in speech.
