The Same News, Twice

One office closure, written for the company and written for a friend — and what changes between them.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

Register is the part of English that textbooks describe last and employers notice first. It is not politeness, exactly, and it is not vocabulary size. It is the set of choices that tells a reader what kind of relationship you think you are in. The clearest way to see it is to take one piece of news and write it twice.

Here is the formal version — a message from a company to its staff.

Following a review of our regional operations, the Board has taken the decision to close the Bristol office with effect from 31 March. All affected colleagues will be offered a role at the Cardiff site or, where relocation is not practicable, a severance package in line with company policy. Individual consultations will commence next week. We recognize that this announcement will be unwelcome, and we are committed to supporting colleagues throughout the transition.

And here is the same news, sent by one of those colleagues to a friend.

So they're shutting Bristol. End of March. Found out this morning. They're saying everyone can move to Cardiff, which is fine if you don't have kids in school here, and otherwise you take the payout. Meetings start next week, one by one, which I'm not looking forward to. Honestly I saw it coming — nobody's replaced anyone here since last summer.

The facts are identical. Everything else has moved. Look first at the verbs. The formal version prefers nouns where the informal one uses verbs: a decision has been taken rather than they decided, consultations will commence rather than meetings start. This is nominalisation, and its effect is to remove people from sentences. Notice that nobody in the formal version actually does anything — the Board takes a decision, but colleagues are offered, and the announcement will be unwelcome without anyone being said to be unhappy about it.

Then the passive voice, which does the same work. “All affected colleagues will be offered a role” — offered by whom? The sentence declines to say, and that is not laziness; it is the point. Formal institutional writing frequently removes the agent from anything difficult, and once you have noticed the pattern you cannot stop seeing it.

Vocabulary shifts too, and predictably: Latin-derived words move up the register and Anglo-Saxon ones move down. Commence rather than start, terminate rather than end, purchase rather than buy, relocation rather than moving, severance package rather than payout. This is a fairly reliable rule in English, and it is the legacy of the Norman conquest, after which the language of law and administration was French and the language of ordinary life was not.

The informal version is doing things the formal one cannot. It has an opinion — “which is fine if you don't have kids in school here”. It admits a feeling: not looking forward to it. It offers an interpretation the company would never print: nobody has been replaced since last summer. And it uses contractions, sentence fragments and a conversational discourse marker, honestly, to signal that this is one person talking to another rather than an institution addressing a category.

Neither version is better. The formal one is doing a job — it has to be accurate, apply equally to four hundred people, and survive being read by a lawyer. The informal one would be inappropriate from a company and is exactly right between friends. What matters is being able to hear which one a situation calls for, because using the wrong one is the error people actually notice: a job application in the register of a message to a friend reads as careless, and a message to a friend in the register of a company announcement reads as cold, or as though something is being hidden.

Key Vocabulary

  • register the level of formality a piece of language is pitched at
  • with effect from starting on the stated date; a formal fixed phrase
  • severance package the money and terms given to an employee who is losing their job
  • payout an informal word for a lump sum of money paid out
  • passive voice a construction in which the thing acted on becomes the subject
  • agent the person or thing performing the action in a sentence
  • to decline to choose not to do something
  • Anglo-Saxon the older Germanic layer of English vocabulary
  • legacy something left behind by an earlier period
  • sentence fragment an incomplete sentence used deliberately, such as “End of March.”
  • discourse marker a word such as honestly or anyway that signals the speaker's attitude
  • to take a decision to decide — the formal collocation, which hides who decided
  • to be committed to to state publicly that you will do something, without promising it

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • “Nobody in the formal version actually does anything.” Find a real announcement and check whether that is true of it.
  • Is removing the agent from difficult news dishonest, or just professional?
  • “Committed to supporting” promises less than “we will support”. What other phrases sound like promises without being them?
  • Which register do you find harder to write in your own second language, and why?
  • Take the informal message in this text and rewrite it as the formal announcement, then compare yours with the one given. What did you change first?
