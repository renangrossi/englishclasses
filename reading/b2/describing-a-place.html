Describing a Place

Four rooms, and what changes when you write down what you can hear and smell instead of what you can see.

Most people describing a place list what they can see, and the result is flat. What makes a written place feel real is usually a sound, a smell, or something physical you would notice with your eyes shut. Here are four, each built around a different sense.

A Kitchen in the Morning

The coffee has been on long enough for the smell to have reached the stairs. Bacon is going in a pan, which is the loudest thing in the room, and under it there is the slower sound of oatmeal moving in a pot. Sunlight comes through thin curtains and lands on the counter. Somebody is cutting fruit, and the knife against the board is the rhythm the rest of it sits on — strawberries, then something harder, a kiwi maybe, which makes a different sound. The coffee machine gives a last splutter and stops. A cup is filled. There is the noise of plates and two people talking, and the specific comfort of a room where breakfast is nearly ready and nobody has to be anywhere for another hour.

A Campsite at Dusk

Pine, first, and then woodsmoke. The fire has got past the crackling stage and settled into the occasional pop. Tents stand in a loose circle, dulled to brown and green in the failing light. There is a stream somewhere close enough to hear and not close enough to see. A cast-iron pan is on a portable stove and whatever is in it has started to catch slightly at the edges, which is not a problem. People are on folding chairs with their faces lit from below, and the conversation has reached the stage where nobody is trying to be interesting. Later somebody gets a guitar out, and later than that the tents glow from the inside as lanterns go on, and the sky over the clearing turns out to have considerably more stars in it than anybody remembered.

A Training Room

The sound in a jiu-jitsu room is bodies hitting a mat, which is duller and heavier than people expect. Bare feet shuffle and slap. The mats smell of rubber and of the disinfectant used on them that morning. Pairs circle each other, and the circling is the longest part; the technique, when it comes, takes about a second and a half. The instructor moves between them correcting grips with two fingers. Everybody's breathing has synchronised without anybody deciding to, and the only voices are short — a name, a laugh, somebody tapping out and both of them resetting. It looks like fighting and behaves like a very physical argument conducted in a language both people have agreed to speak.

A Shopping Center at Six

Then the opposite of all three. The shopping center after work is noise with no pattern in it: escalators, tills, several hundred conversations, and a track playing somewhere that nobody is listening to. The air is warm and carries coffee, perfume and the particular smell of new clothes. Everybody is walking slightly faster than is comfortable and apologizing without looking up. The queue at the checkout is the one place the pace breaks, and people standing in it look at each other for the first time — somebody's child asleep against their shoulder, somebody holding a single item and visibly regretting the whole trip. Ten minutes later you are outside with a bag in each hand, and the quiet of the car park is startling.

Notice what the four have in common. Almost nothing is described by color. What is doing the work is sound — the knife, the fire, the mat, the escalators — and smell, which is the sense writers use least and readers trust most. Notice also that each one contains a small human observation that could not have been guessed from the setting: nobody having to be anywhere for an hour, nobody trying to be interesting, the argument conducted in an agreed language, the man regretting the trip. That is the difference between describing a place and listing its contents.

Vocabulary

  • flat — dull and lifeless, said of writing
  • to go — to be cooking noisily
  • board — the wooden or plastic surface you cut food on
  • splutter — a short burst of broken, spitting sound
  • woodsmoke — the smell of a wood fire
  • to crackle — to make small sharp popping sounds
  • dulled — made less bright
  • failing light — the last of the daylight, as it goes
  • to catch — to begin to burn slightly on the bottom of a pan
  • clearing — an open space in a forest
  • mat — the thick padded floor covering used in martial arts
  • to shuffle — to move the feet without lifting them properly
  • disinfectant — the cleaning liquid used to kill germs
  • grip — the way you take hold of something, or of somebody
  • to tap out — to signal surrender by tapping, in grappling sports
  • to reset — to return to the starting position and begin again
  • till — the machine that records sales and holds the money
  • pace — the speed at which something moves
  • to break — to stop, said of a pattern or a rhythm
  • startling — surprising enough to make you stop
  • to list — to name one after another without connecting them

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Describe the room you are in now in five sentences, without naming a single color.
  • “The sense writers use least and readers trust most.” Why do you think smell works that way?
  • Each scene has one observation that could not be guessed from the setting. Find them, and say which is the best.
  • Which of the four places would you most want to be in right now, and what detail decided it?
  • Write about 200 words describing a place you know well. Use at least three sounds, one smell, and one small observation about the people in it.
