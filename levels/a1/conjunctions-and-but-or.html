Conjunctions: And, But, Or

Connect ideas with the three most common linking words.

ObjectivesExplanationRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummaryRelated

Introduction

And, but and or are the simplest way to connect two ideas in English. Each one signals a different relationship between the ideas it joins.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Use “and” to add information
  • Use “but” to show contrast
  • Use “or” to show a choice or alternative

Explanation

Explanation

And simply adds one idea to another: I like apples and oranges. But introduces a contrast or an unexpected turn: I like apples, but I don't like oranges.

Or presents a choice between two or more options: Do you want tea or coffee? Notice the comma before “but” when it joins two full sentences.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

  • And = addition (I like apples and oranges.)
  • But = contrast (I like apples, but I don't like oranges.)
  • Or = choice / alternative (Tea or coffee?)
  • Use a comma before “but” when joining two complete sentences

Examples

See It in Action

  • She is smart and friendly.
  • He wanted to go, but it was raining.
  • Would you like water or juice?
  • I studied hard, but I still felt nervous.
  • We can walk or take the bus.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidI like tea but coffee.

Use insteadI like tea or coffee.

A choice between two things needs “or,” not “but.”

AvoidShe is tall or friendly.

Use insteadShe is tall and friendly.

Two compatible qualities are simply added together with “and,” not “or.”

AvoidIt was cold, and I still went out.

Use insteadIt was cold, but I still went out.

An unexpected contrast needs “but,” not “and.”

Tip

Ask yourself: am I adding (and), contrasting (but), or choosing (or)? The right conjunction usually follows naturally.

Review
