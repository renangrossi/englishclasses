Native-Like Use of Modality and Attitude

Introduction

C2 modality goes beyond certainty/possibility to convey fine shades of tentativeness, concession, politeness and layered epistemic stance — how confident, cautious or diplomatic the speaker wants to sound.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Understand and use: Subtle modal shading
  • Understand and use: Layered modality — combining more than one modal expression (typically a modal + a modal-like verb phrase such as "expected to," "supposed to," "meant to") to express a nuanced, sometimes ironic, stance on an obligation or expectation that itself involves uncertainty.
  • Understand and use: Epistemic stance markers — phrases that signal how the writer/speaker is framing their own certainty, common in academic and diplomatic register.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Subtle modal shading

  • Tentativeness/hedging: might, could, would seem to, it could be said that, one might argue. It would seem that the strategy has largely failed. (softer, more diplomatic than "The strategy has failed")
  • Concession within modality: may/might + well (acknowledging a point is likely true, often before contrasting it): She might well be right, but I still have reservations.
  • Politeness distancing: would/could/might used to soften requests, suggestions or opinions, creating social distance and respect. Might I suggest an alternative approach? (very formal/polite) vs. Can I suggest...? (neutral)

b) Layered modality — combining more than one modal expression (typically a modal + a modal-like verb phrase such as "expected to," "supposed to," "meant to") to express a nuanced, sometimes ironic, stance on an obligation or expectation that itself involves uncertainty.

He might have been expected to apologize, but he never did. (= it would have been reasonable to expect an apology, though uncertainty/distance is baked in)

She should, in theory, have been informed beforehand.

c) Epistemic stance markers — phrases that signal how the writer/speaker is framing their own certainty, common in academic and diplomatic register.

It is conceivable that... / There is reason to believe that... / One cannot rule out the possibility that... / It stands to reason that...

Native-like control means choosing the modal combination that matches exactly how certain, cautious, or polite you intend to sound — not just whether something is possible or necessary.

Examples

See It in Action

  • It would seem that the negotiations have reached a stalemate.
  • She might well be the most qualified candidate, but the panel remains divided.
  • Might I suggest we revisit this proposal at a later date?
  • He might have been expected to resign after the scandal, yet he remains in office.
  • One cannot rule out the possibility that the results were affected by bias.
  • There is reason to believe that the original estimate was overly optimistic.
  • It could be argued that the reform, while well-intentioned, was poorly executed.
  • She should, in theory, have received the documents by now.
  • It stands to reason that costs would rise given the current shortages.
  • He may well have a point, but the timing of his objection is questionable.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidIt would seem the strategy fails.

Use insteadIt would seem that the strategy has failed.

This hedged structure typically requires "that" before the clause, and the verb tense should match the intended time frame (here, a completed failure).

AvoidShe might well being right.

Use insteadShe might well be right.

After "might well," use the bare infinitive ("be"), not the gerund.

AvoidHe might have expected to apologize.

Use insteadHe might have been expected to apologize.

"Be expected to" is a passive structure (others expected it of him); dropping "been" changes the meaning to "he expected himself."

AvoidIt cannot rule out the possibility that costs will rise.

Use insteadOne cannot rule out the possibility that costs will rise.

The impersonal subject here is "one" (or "we"), not "it" — "it" doesn't fit this fixed epistemic phrase.

AvoidMight I to suggest an alternative?

Use insteadMight I suggest an alternative?

After the inverted modal "Might I," use the bare infinitive directly, without "to."
