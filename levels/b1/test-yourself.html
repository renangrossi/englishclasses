Topic 1 of 9

Past Simple vs. Present Perfect I

Know when the exact time matters, and when it doesn't.

  • Past simple + specific time (yesterday, last year, in 2020)
  • Present perfect + unspecified time (ever, already, yet, so far)
  • Once a specific past time is mentioned, switch to past simple
  • Present perfect keeps the connection to now open

AvoidI have visited Paris in 2019.

Use insteadI visited Paris in 2019.

A specific past time (2019) needs the simple past, not the present perfect.

AvoidShe called me already.

Use insteadShe has already called me.

“Already,” without a specific time, typically signals the present perfect.

AvoidI have finished the project last month.

Use insteadI finished the project last month.

“Last month” is a specific finished time, so it needs the simple past.

Topic 2 of 9

Past Simple vs. Present Perfect II

More practice with this key contrast, in mixed real-world contexts.

  • Same core rule: specific time → past simple; unspecified → present perfect
  • Conversations often move from present perfect (general question) to past simple (specific follow-up)
  • Watch for hidden time references (this morning, already today)

AvoidDid you ever visit Egypt?

Use insteadHave you ever visited Egypt?

“Ever,” without a specific time, signals the present perfect, not the simple past.

AvoidHave you called her yesterday?

Use insteadDid you call her yesterday?

“Yesterday” is a specific past time, so the question needs the simple past.

AvoidI have seen that movie last week.

Use insteadI saw that movie last week.

A specific time (last week) needs the simple past, even though the general question form might have used the present perfect.

Topic 3 of 9

Present Perfect Continuous

Focus on an activity that started in the past and continues now.

  • Have/has been + verb-ing
  • Emphasizes duration or a recent, ongoing activity
  • Explains a present result from a recent activity
  • Not usually used with state verbs, or with a specific finished quantity

AvoidI have been reading three books this month.

Use insteadI have read three books this month.

A specific finished quantity (three books) fits the simple present perfect, not the continuous.

AvoidShe has been knowing him for years.

Use insteadShe has known him for years.

“Know” is a state verb and doesn't take the continuous form.

AvoidI been waiting for an hour.

Use insteadI have been waiting for an hour.

The auxiliary “have” cannot be dropped: have been waiting.

Topic 4 of 9

Present Perfect vs. Present Perfect Continuous

Choose between a completed result and an ongoing activity.

  • Present perfect = have/has + past participle (result/completion)
  • Present perfect continuous = have/has been + verb-ing (ongoing activity)
  • For + a period of time (for two hours, for ten years)
  • Since + a starting point (since 2020, since Monday)

AvoidI have been knowing her for years.

Use insteadI have known her for years.

“Know” is a state verb and doesn't normally take the continuous form.

AvoidShe has finished her homework since 2 hours.

Use insteadShe has finished her homework for 2 hours / She finished her homework 2 hours ago.

“Since” needs a starting point in time, not a duration — use “for” with a duration.

AvoidI have live here since ten years.

Use insteadI have lived here for ten years.

The past participle needs -d: lived. And a duration (ten years) needs “for,” not “since.”

Topic 5 of 9

Past Simple vs. Past Perfect

Show which of two past events happened first.

  • Past perfect: had + past participle
  • Used for the earlier of two past actions
  • Past simple describes the later action, or a single past event
  • Often combined with before, after, when, by the time

AvoidWhen I arrived, she left already.

Use insteadWhen I arrived, she had already left.

The earlier of two past actions needs the past perfect: had left.

AvoidI had eaten breakfast this morning (only one past event).

Use insteadI ate breakfast this morning.

With only one past event, the simple past is enough — no need for the past perfect.

AvoidBy the time we arrived, the film already started.

Use insteadBy the time we arrived, the film had already started.

The earlier event (starting) needs the past perfect: had started.

Topic 6 of 9

Must, Might, Should

Express obligation, possibility and advice.

  • Must = strong obligation / logical certainty
  • Might = possibility
  • Should = advice / recommendation / expectation
  • All followed by the base verb, no “to”

AvoidHe must to be tired.

Use insteadHe must be tired.

No “to” after modal verbs: must be, not must to be.

AvoidYou should to see a doctor.

Use insteadYou should see a doctor.

No “to” after “should” either: should see.

AvoidIt should rain later (uncertain possibility).

Use insteadIt might rain later.

An uncertain possibility needs “might,” not “should,” which implies expectation or advice.

Topic 7 of 9

Second Conditional

Talk about unreal present and future situations.

  • Second conditional: If + past simple, would + base verb (unreal present/future)
  • “Were” is often used instead of “was” in the if-clause: If I were you…

AvoidIf I would have more time, I would learn a language.

Use insteadIf I had more time, I would learn a language.

The if-clause of a second conditional uses the past simple, not “would have.”

AvoidIf I was you, I would apologize.

Use insteadIf I were you, I would apologize.

“Were” (not “was”) is the traditional form used with “I/he/she/it” in hypothetical if-clauses.

Topic 8 of 9

Passive Voice

Shift the focus from who did something to what happened.

  • Be (in the right tense) + past participle
  • Object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive
  • By + agent, only when the doer matters
  • Used when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or obvious from context

AvoidThe cake baked by my mother.

Use insteadThe cake is baked by my mother.

The passive needs the auxiliary “be” in the right tense: is baked.

AvoidThe house was build in 1990.

Use insteadThe house was built in 1990.

The past participle of “build” is “built,” not “build.”

AvoidThe results will announced tomorrow.

Use insteadThe results will be announced tomorrow.

The passive with “will” needs “be” before the past participle: will be announced.

Topic 9 of 9

Question Tags

Add a short question to confirm or check information.

  • Positive statement → negative tag
  • Negative statement → positive tag
  • The tag repeats the sentence's auxiliary verb (is, do, can, will…)
  • The tag always uses a pronoun, never the original noun

AvoidYou are tired, are you?

Use insteadYou are tired, aren't you?

A positive statement needs a negative tag, not another positive one.

AvoidShe doesn't like tea, doesn't she?

Use insteadShe doesn't like tea, does she?

A negative statement needs a positive tag.

AvoidMaria is late, isn't Maria?

Use insteadMaria is late, isn't she?

The tag always uses a pronoun, never repeats the noun.
