Three Days in New York

A family arrives with a long list and works out what you can actually fit into a short trip.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

Lucas had wanted to see New York for as long as he could remember — the height of it, the noise, the food, the sense that something was happening two streets away that he was missing. Now he was standing in a hotel lobby in Manhattan with his wife Rachel and their son Ben, holding three suitcases and a printed list of things to do that was plainly too long for three days.

He asked the clerk at the desk where they could eat nearby without spending a fortune. She did not hesitate. There was a pizzeria a few streets down called Joe's, she said, that had been there for decades and was where people who actually lived in the neighborhood went. A slice, eaten standing up, folded in half lengthways so it does not collapse. That was the correct introduction to the city, and it would cost them almost nothing.

Then she gave him a map and marked the landmarks on it. The Statue of Liberty, the Empire State Building, Central Park. The Metropolitan Museum of Art and the American Museum of Natural History, if they liked museums. Lucas thanked her and folded the map into his pocket, already aware that they were not going to manage all of it.

Rachel said as much on the way to the pizzeria. “We have three days. That list is a week.”

“I know,” Lucas said. “We pick. We can do the park properly, or we can do four things badly.”

“Dinosaurs,” said Ben, who had not appeared to be listening.

So that was one decision made. The pizza, when it came, was enormous and cost less than a coffee had at the airport, and Ben ate his and half of Rachel's.

They gave the next morning to Central Park, and it turned out to be the right call. The park is far larger than it looks on a map — you can walk for an hour and still be inside it — and what struck all three of them was how ordinary it was. Dogs being walked, children on scooters, four different musicians within half a mile, people asleep on the grass in the middle of a working Tuesday. They found the Bethesda Fountain, photographed it along with everyone else, and then sat on the steps for twenty minutes doing nothing at all, which was not on the list and was the best part of the morning.

Afterwards they stopped at a café, and Lucas took the map out again and put his finger on the Empire State Building. Rachel looked at it and said it was closer than it appeared. They asked the man behind the counter, who told them they could take the subway, take a taxi, or walk it in about twenty-five minutes if they did not mind the crowds on Fifth Avenue.

They walked. That turned out to be the second good decision, because the walk was the city: the noise, the scaffolding, the six languages within one block, the way the buildings close in and then suddenly open out at an intersection.

At the top of the Empire State Building the wind was much stronger than any of them expected, and the view was worth the queue. Lucas took more photographs than he needed. Ben, who had been promised dinosaurs the following day, leaned on the rail and asked how long it would take to walk to the edge of the city, which none of them could answer.

Going back down in the lift, Lucas did the arithmetic and realized that in a day and a half they had done three things on a list of fifteen. He found he did not mind. They had seen a fountain, eaten a slice standing up, and walked twenty-five blocks through the middle of it, and the list would still be there next time.

Key Vocabulary

  • lobby the entrance hall of a hotel or large building
  • clerk an employee who deals with customers at a desk
  • to spend a fortune to spend a very large amount of money
  • pizzeria a restaurant that specializes in pizza
  • slice a single flat piece cut from something larger
  • lengthways along the longer direction of something
  • to collapse to fall down or give way
  • landmark a building or feature that is easily recognized and used to find your way
  • to manage to succeed in doing something difficult
  • the right call the correct decision, seen afterwards
  • to strike someone to make a strong impression on someone
  • scaffolding the temporary framework put up around a building under repair
  • queue a line of people waiting
  • rail a bar you can hold on to or lean against
  • block the distance between two street corners in an American city

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Lucas chooses to do a few things properly rather than many badly. Which kind of traveler are you?
  • The best part of the morning was not on the list. Can that be planned for, or only allowed for?
  • The family asks three different strangers for advice. Who gives the best recommendations in a city you know — hotel staff, locals, or the internet?
  • The same pizzeria appears in the A2 text “Ordering at a New York Pizzeria”, where a family orders and works out the tip. Read both: what does the dialogue show that this narrative does not?
  • Write about 150 words about a trip where you did not manage everything you planned. Was that a failure?
