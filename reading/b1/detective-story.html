The False Wall

A safe is emptied, a note is found, and the person nobody suspected turns out to have done it.

It was a dreary Monday morning when Detective Julia Kendall was called out to a burglary. The house belonged to a wealthy businessman, and he had woken to find his safe standing open and emptied — a considerable amount of cash, and his wife's jewellery.

What struck Kendall first was that nothing else had been touched. There was no broken glass, no forced door, nothing disturbed in any other room. Whoever had done this had walked in, gone straight to the safe, and left. That narrowed the list of possibilities immediately, because it meant two things: the burglar had known where the safe was, and had known how to open it.

On the floor near the safe she found a scrap of paper, torn and crumpled, with a few words still legible: “Meet at the park. Richard.” She bagged it without much confidence. It could have been months old, and it named a first name and nothing else.

The name turned out to matter. As she went through the household's recent history, she learned that the businessman had dismissed his personal assistant three months earlier — a man named Richard — after money had gone missing from a company account. The businessman had chosen not to press charges, on the grounds that it would take a year and achieve nothing, and had simply let him go. Two of the staff remembered Richard being furious, and one recalled him saying he would get even.

It was suggestive, and it was not evidence. Kendall had a former employee with a grudge, a note with a common first name, and a safe that had been opened by someone who knew the combination. None of that would survive ten minutes in a courtroom.

What broke it was a phone call. A man named Douglas, who had worked alongside Richard and had kept in touch, rang to say that he had seen him at the weekend wearing a watch he was certain belonged to the businessman's wife — he had noticed it years earlier because of an unusual green face. He sounded reluctant on the phone, and said twice that he hoped he was wrong.

That was enough for a warrant. Kendall and two officers went to Richard's flat on the Thursday morning. They found nothing in the obvious places, and were almost finished when one of the officers noticed that a kitchen cupboard was a good deal shallower inside than the wall behind it should have allowed. Behind it was a cavity, and in the cavity were the cash, the jewellery, and — folded into a jacket pocket — the other half of the torn note.

Richard denied everything for the first twenty minutes of the interview. He stopped when Kendall put the two halves of the paper on the table in front of him and did not say anything at all. Then he asked whether it would help him to explain, and by the end of the afternoon he had admitted all of it.

What surprised people afterwards was not that the money had gone. It was who had taken it. Richard had coached a junior football team for six years, and several of the neighbours interviewed said, in almost the same words, that he was the last person they would have expected. Kendall had heard that sentence at the end of a great many cases, and had stopped finding it remarkable. She wrote up the file and went back to her desk, where there were four more.

Vocabulary

  • dreary — dull, grey and depressing
  • burglary — the crime of entering a building illegally to steal
  • safe — a locked metal box or cabinet for valuables
  • to strike someone — to make an immediate impression on someone
  • forced — broken open by force
  • to narrow — to reduce the number of possibilities
  • scrap — a small torn piece of something
  • crumpled — crushed into folds and creases
  • legible — clear enough to be read
  • to bag — to seal evidence in a bag at a crime scene
  • to dismiss — to remove someone from their job
  • to press charges — to make a formal accusation so that a prosecution can follow
  • grudge — a lasting resentment against someone
  • to get even — to take revenge
  • suggestive — pointing toward a conclusion without proving it
  • warrant — a court document authorising a search or an arrest
  • cavity — a hollow space inside something solid
  • reluctant — unwilling; doing something only with hesitation
  • to coach — to train a sports team
  • to write up — to produce the finished written record of something

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The businessman decided not to press charges the first time. Was that a reasonable decision, given what happened afterwards?
  • Douglas was reluctant to report a former colleague. What would you have done?
  • Kendall separates what is suggestive from what is evidence. Why does that distinction matter outside a courtroom too?
  • “The last person they would have expected.” Why do people say this so often about someone who turns out to be guilty?
  • Write about 150 words continuing the story: Kendall opens one of the four remaining files. What is in it?
