A2

Prepositions of Place & Movement

Say exactly where something is, and where it is going.

ObjectivesExplanationRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummaryTest Yourself

Introduction

At A1 you learned in, on and at. This lesson adds the words that say exactly where something is — and the ones that say where it is going.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Say where something is with in front of, behind, next to, between, among, above, below, under, near and far from
  • Say where something goes with into, out of, onto, off, through, across, over, along and towards
  • Choose between position and movement: in / into, on / onto
  • Tell apart the pairs that are easy to confuse: between / among, across / through, under / below

Explanation

Explanation

English separates two ideas that many languages express with the same word. A preposition of place answers “Where is it?” and usually follows a verb with no movement in it: be, sit, stand, live, wait. A preposition of movement answers “Where is it going?” and follows a verb of motion: go, walk, run, jump, drive, fly, climb.

Some words come in pairs, one for each idea: the cat is in the box, but it jumped into the box.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Place — where something is

  • in front of / behind: The cat is sitting in front of the TV. / There’s a garden behind the school.
  • next to (also beside): The pharmacy is next to the bakery.
  • between: I sit between Ana and Leo.
  • among: She found her ring among the leaves.
  • above / below — higher or lower, not touching: A drone is flying above the house. / We live on the floor below yours.
  • under — directly beneath, often covered by it: The keys are under the sofa.
  • near (also close to) / far from: Our hotel is near the beach. / The airport is far from the city.

b) Movement — where something goes

  • into / out of — entering and leaving: The dog jumped into the lake. / She took a pen out of her bag.
  • onto / off — to the top of something, and away from it: The cat climbed onto the roof. / He fell off his bike.
  • through — in one side and out the other, with something around you: We drove through the tunnel.
  • across — from one side of a surface to the other: We swam across the lake.
  • over — above something and to the other side: The ball flew over the wall.
  • along — following the length of something: They walked along the river.
  • towards — in the direction of: He ran towards the exit.

c) Position or movement? in / into, on / onto

  • in, on say where something is: The milk is in the fridge. / The books are on the shelf.
  • into, onto say where something goes: She walked into the room. / The cat jumped onto the table.
  • The difference can change the meaning: He ran in the park (he was there, running) is not He ran into the park (he entered it).
  • In everyday speech, people often say in and on after verbs like jump, put and fall (“She jumped in the pool”). Into and onto are never wrong there, and they make the movement clear.

d) Pairs that are easy to confuse

  • between / among: use between for separate things or people you can name or count, usually two — between the bank and the school, between Ana and Leo. Use among for the middle of a group you are not counting one by one — among the trees, among friends.
  • across / through: across is over a surface, side to side — across the street, the bridge, the river. Through is with something around you — through a tunnel, a forest, a door, a crowd. A park can take both: across the park is from one side to the other; through the park is in among its trees and paths.
  • under / below: under is directly beneath — under the bed, under the bridge. Below is simply lower — the floor below, below zero.
  • along / across: you walk along a river if you follow it, and across it if you go to the other side.

Examples

See It in Action

  • The keys are under the sofa.
  • I sit between Ana and Leo.
  • She found her ring among the leaves.
  • We live on the floor below yours.
  • The dog jumped into the lake.
  • She took a pen out of her bag.
  • We drove through the tunnel and then across the bridge.
  • They walked along the river towards the old town.
  • The ball flew over the wall.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidThe bus stop is in front the bank.

Use insteadThe bus stop is in front of the bank.

“In front of” is three words. So are “next to” and “out of”: none of them works without its last word.

AvoidWe live near of the station.

Use insteadWe live near the station.

“Near” takes no “of” and no “to.” If you want “to,” say “close to the station.”

AvoidShe took her phone out her bag.

Use insteadShe took her phone out of her bag.

Leaving a container is “out of”: out of the bag, out of the car, out of the room.

AvoidWe walked through the bridge.

Use insteadWe walked across the bridge.

A bridge is a surface you cross: across. “Through” needs something around you, like a tunnel.

AvoidThey are sitting into the car.

Use insteadThey are sitting in the car.

“Sit” here has no movement in it, so the position word is “in.” “Into” needs a verb of motion: They got into the car.

AvoidThe lamp is among the two chairs.

Use insteadThe lamp is between the two chairs.

Two separate things, one on each side: between.

Summary

Review

  • Place answers “Where is it?”: in front of, behind, next to, between, among, above, below, under, near, far from.
  • Movement answers “Where is it going?”: into, out of, onto, off, through, across, over, along, towards.
  • In and on give a position; into and onto show movement — He ran in the park / He ran into the park.
  • Between is for separate things you can name, usually two; among is for the middle of a group.
  • Across is over a surface; through is with something around you; along follows the length.
  • In front of, next to and out of never lose their last word.
