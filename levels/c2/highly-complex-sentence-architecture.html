Introduction

C2 academic and literary writing often uses long sentences that nest several subordinate clauses, front entire clauses for emphasis, and pack dense noun phrases — all while remaining grammatically clear.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Understand and use: Nested subordinate clauses — a main clause can contain a relative clause, which itself contains another clause, building layers of information.
  • Understand and use: Multi-clause fronting — moving an entire subordinate clause (or more than one) to the front of the sentence for emphasis, cohesion with the previous sentence, or rhythm.
  • Understand and use: High-density noun phrases and academic phrasing — combining nominalization, multiple modifiers and embedded clauses into a single compact subject or object.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Nested subordinate clauses — a main clause can contain a relative clause, which itself contains another clause, building layers of information.

The report, which the committee — most of whom had opposed the original plan — reluctantly approved, outlined a strategy that few believed would work.

b) Multi-clause fronting — moving an entire subordinate clause (or more than one) to the front of the sentence for emphasis, cohesion with the previous sentence, or rhythm.

Although the data seemed conclusive, and although several experts had endorsed the findings, the board remained unconvinced.

What had once seemed an impossible goal, given the constraints they faced, now looked entirely achievable.

c) High-density noun phrases and academic phrasing — combining nominalization, multiple modifiers and embedded clauses into a single compact subject or object.

The steady erosion of public trust in institutions once considered unimpeachable has reshaped modern political discourse.

Key architectural principles at C2:

  • Use dashes or parentheses to insert a clause without breaking the main sentence's flow.
  • Keep the main verb clearly identifiable even in a long sentence — readers should never lose track of the core subject-verb relationship.
  • Balance clause length: extremely long sentences need at least one shorter sentence nearby to maintain readability.

Examples

See It in Action

  • The proposal, which had been debated for months and which several board members privately opposed, was finally approved.
  • Although the results were promising, and although the funding had already been secured, the project was ultimately shelved.
  • What had once been dismissed as a fringe theory is now widely accepted within the scientific community.
  • The gradual decline in voter turnout, a trend observed across most Western democracies, has troubled political scientists for decades.
  • Having reviewed the evidence — evidence that, incidentally, contradicted the official account — the judge dismissed the case.
  • That she succeeded despite every obstacle placed in her path is a testament to her resilience.
  • The very policies designed to protect small businesses have, ironically, made survival harder for many of them.
  • Whatever conclusions one draws from the data, and however one interprets the underlying trends, the overall pattern remains undeniable.
  • It was not until the final report was published, months after the initial allegations surfaced, that the full scale of the scandal became clear.
  • The committee's decision, reached only after weeks of heated debate, satisfied almost no one.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidThe report, which the committee approved it, outlined a new strategy.

Use insteadThe report, which the committee approved, outlined a new strategy.

Don't repeat the object with a redundant pronoun ("it") once a relative pronoun ("which") already fills that role.

AvoidAlthough the data seemed conclusive, but the board remained unconvinced.

Use insteadAlthough the data seemed conclusive, the board remained unconvinced.

"Although" and "but" cannot both introduce the contrast — choose one.

AvoidWhat had once seemed impossible, now looked achievable it.

Use insteadWhat had once seemed impossible now looked achievable.

No comma is needed between a fronted "what" clause functioning as the subject and the main verb; and don't add a redundant "it" at the end.

AvoidThe decline in trust, that scholars have studied extensively, reshaped politics.

Use insteadThe decline in trust, which scholars have studied extensively, reshaped politics.

In a non-defining (comma-separated) relative clause, use "which," not "that."

AvoidHaving reviewed the evidence, the case were dismissed by the judge.

Use insteadHaving reviewed the evidence, the judge dismissed the case.

The subject right after a participle clause ("Having reviewed...") must be the one performing that action — here, the judge, not the case.
