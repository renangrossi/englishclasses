Finding an Apartment

Dani has three weeks, a budget and a list of things she will not give up.

Dani is moving to Denver for a new job, and she has three weeks to find a place to live.

Her budget is fourteen hundred dollars a month. In Denver that is not a lot, so she makes a list of what she really needs. The apartment must be near a bus line, because she does not have a car. It must allow cats. And it must have a washing machine in the building, because she does not want to spend every Saturday at a laundromat.

Everything else can go. She looks at the list and crosses off “big kitchen”.

The advertisements use words she has to learn. A studio is one room with a kitchen in it. A one-bedroom has a separate bedroom. Utilities included means the price covers water, heat and electricity — and when it does not say that, the real cost is higher than the number in the advertisement. Deposit is the money you pay at the start and get back at the end, if nothing is broken.

She sees four apartments in two days. The first is cheap and dark. The second is beautiful and six hundred dollars over her budget. The third smells of smoke. The fourth is small, on the third floor, with no elevator, and it has a window facing west and a bus stop at the corner.

She asks the questions she has written down. Are utilities included? Yes, except electricity. Is there a laundry room? In the basement. Can she have a cat? The landlord thinks about it and says yes, with an extra deposit of three hundred dollars.

She takes it. It is not the apartment she imagined in January. It is twenty minutes from work, it costs thirteen hundred and fifty, and the afternoon light in that west window is very good.

She signs the lease on a Thursday. The hardest part, she says afterwards, was not the money. It was deciding which of the things on her list were real.

Vocabulary

  • budget — the amount of money you can spend
  • bus line — a route that buses follow
  • to allow — to say that something is permitted
  • laundromat — a shop with washing machines you pay to use — American
  • to cross off — to remove something from a list
  • studio — an apartment that is one room with a kitchen in it
  • utilities — water, heat, gas and electricity, as a cost
  • deposit — money paid at the start and returned at the end if nothing is damaged
  • elevator — the machine that carries you between floors — American; British lift
  • to face — to look out in a certain direction
  • basement — the floor below the ground floor
  • landlord — the person who owns the apartment you rent
  • lease — the contract you sign to rent a home

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Do you rent or own your home? How did you find it?
  • What are the three things you would not give up in a home?
  • In your city, does the rent usually include water and electricity?
  • What would you cross off your list if the budget were smaller?
