Topic 1 of 14

Present Simple vs. Present Continuous

Know when to use routines vs. actions in progress.

  • Simple present = routines, habits, facts
  • Present continuous = happening now, or temporary
  • Simple present time words: always, usually, every day
  • Present continuous time words: now, at the moment, currently
  • Some verbs (like, know, want) are rarely used in the continuous form

AvoidI am knowing the answer.

Use insteadI know the answer.

“Know” describes a state, not an action, so it doesn't normally take the continuous form.

AvoidShe working now.

Use insteadShe is working now.

The present continuous needs the auxiliary “is/am/are” before the -ing verb.

AvoidHe is usually arriving late.

Use insteadHe usually arrives late.

“Usually” signals a routine, so it needs the simple present, not the continuous.

Topic 2 of 14

Adverbs of Frequency

Say how often something happens, in the right position.

  • Before the main verb: I always eat breakfast.
  • After “to be”: She is often tired.
  • “How often…?” asks about frequency
  • Never and rarely already carry a negative meaning — don't add “not”

AvoidI eat always breakfast.

Use insteadI always eat breakfast.

The frequency adverb goes before the main verb, not after it.

AvoidShe always is late.

Use insteadShe is always late.

With “to be,” the frequency adverb goes after the verb.

AvoidI don't never eat meat.

Use insteadI never eat meat.

“Never” already means “not ever” — adding “don't” creates a double negative.

Topic 3 of 14

Quantifiers

Say how much or how many, correctly for each noun type.

  • Many/few + countable nouns (many books, few ideas)
  • Much/little + uncountable nouns (much water, little information)
  • A lot of works with both countable and uncountable nouns
  • Some → affirmative sentences; any → questions and negatives

AvoidHow much books do you have?

Use insteadHow many books do you have?

“Books” is countable, so it needs “many,” not “much.”

AvoidI don't have some money.

Use insteadI don't have any money.

Negative sentences use “any,” not “some.”

AvoidThere is much apples on the table.

Use insteadThere are many apples on the table.

“Apples” is countable and plural, so it needs “many” and “are.”

Topic 4 of 14

Comparatives and Superlatives

Compare two things, or single out the best or worst of a group.

  • Short adjectives: add -er / -est (tall → taller → tallest)
  • Long adjectives: more / most + adjective
  • Adjectives ending in -y: change y to -ier / -iest (happy → happier)
  • Comparative + than; the + superlative
  • Irregular: good → better → best; bad → worse → worst

AvoidThis is more big than that.

Use insteadThis is bigger than that.

“Big” is a short adjective, so it takes -er, not “more.”

AvoidShe is the most tall in class.

Use insteadShe is the tallest in class.

Short adjectives use -est for the superlative, not “most.”

AvoidHe is gooder than me.

Use insteadHe is better than me.

“Good” is irregular: better, not gooder.

Topic 5 of 14

Prepositions of Place and Movement

Describe where things are, and how things move.

  • Place: in, on, under, between, behind, in front of, next to
  • Movement: to, into, onto, through, across, along, past
  • In = already inside; into = moving to the inside
  • On = already on top; onto = moving to the top

AvoidShe walked in the room.

Use insteadShe walked into the room.

Movement toward the inside of a place needs “into,” not just “in.”

AvoidThe cat jumped on the table (from the floor).

Use insteadThe cat jumped onto the table.

Movement toward the top of a surface needs “onto.”

AvoidHe is between the two buildings (static, correct)

Use insteadHe is between the two buildings.

This one is already correct — “between” two things is a static place preposition.

Topic 6 of 14

Past Simple and Past Continuous

Combine a finished action with one that was in progress.

  • Past continuous: was/were + verb-ing
  • Used for an action in progress at a specific past time
  • Interrupted action: past continuous + when + past simple
  • While + past continuous; when + past simple

AvoidI was sleep when you called.

Use insteadI was sleeping when you called.

The past continuous needs the -ing form: sleeping, not sleep.

AvoidWhile I cooked, the phone rang.

Use insteadWhile I was cooking, the phone rang.

“While” usually introduces the ongoing action, which needs the past continuous.

AvoidI watched TV when the phone was ringing.

Use insteadI was watching TV when the phone rang.

The ongoing action (watching) needs the continuous; the interrupting action (rang) needs the simple past.

Topic 7 of 14

Can, Could, May

Express ability, permission and possibility.

  • Can + base verb = present ability / informal permission
  • Could + base verb = past ability / polite request
  • May + base verb = formal permission / possibility
  • Never add “to” after can/could/may

AvoidI can to swim.

Use insteadI can swim.

Modal verbs like “can” are followed directly by the base verb, with no “to.”

AvoidShe can swam when she was young.

Use insteadShe could swim when she was young.

Past ability needs “could,” not “can” + past tense verb.

AvoidMay you help me?

Use insteadCould you help me?

“May” isn't typically used to ask someone else for help — “Could” is the natural choice for requests.

Topic 8 of 14

Zero and First Conditionals

Talk about general truths and likely future results.

  • Zero conditional: If + present simple, present simple (general truths)
  • First conditional: If + present simple, will + base verb (likely future)
  • The if-clause can come first or second: If it rains, we'll stay in. / We'll stay in if it rains.
  • Never use “will” in the if-clause itself

AvoidIf it will rain, we will stay home.

Use insteadIf it rains, we will stay home.

The if-clause uses the present simple, not “will,” even though the sentence is about the future.

AvoidIf you heat ice, it will melt (as a general fact).

Use insteadIf you heat ice, it melts.

A general scientific fact needs the zero conditional (present simple + present simple), not “will.”

AvoidIf she will study, she will pass.

Use insteadIf she studies, she will pass.

“Will” never appears in the if-clause of a first conditional.

Topic 9 of 14

Relative Pronouns: Who, Which, That

Add extra information to a noun with a relative clause.

  • Who → people
  • Which → things and animals
  • That → people or things, informal, defining clauses only
  • The relative pronoun comes right after the noun it describes

AvoidThe book who I read was great.

Use insteadThe book that I read was great.

“Book” is a thing, not a person, so it needs “which” or “that,” not “who.”

AvoidThe man which called is my father.

Use insteadThe man who called is my father.

“Man” is a person, so it needs “who,” not “which.”

AvoidShe is the girl which I met yesterday.

Use insteadShe is the girl who I met yesterday.

“Girl” is a person, so it needs “who.”

Topic 10 of 14

Connectors: Addition, Contrast, Cause

Link ideas clearly with the right connecting word.

  • Addition: also, besides, as well as, in addition
  • Contrast: but, however, although, even though
  • Cause: because + reason
  • Result: so / therefore + consequence

AvoidAlthough it was raining, but we went out.

Use insteadAlthough it was raining, we went out.

Don't use “although” and “but” together — pick one contrast connector.

AvoidI was tired because I went to bed.

Use insteadI went to bed because I was tired.

“Because” introduces the reason, which needs to come logically before the result.

AvoidIt was cold, because we stayed inside.

Use insteadIt was cold, so we stayed inside.

The result (staying inside) needs “so,” not “because,” which introduces a cause.

Topic 11 of 14

Simple Past vs. Past Continuous

Choose the right past tense for the situation.

  • Simple past = sequence of completed actions
  • Past continuous = background scene, or already in progress
  • Two past simple verbs joined by “and” = actions in sequence
  • Past continuous + when + past simple = interruption

AvoidI was waking up, ate breakfast, and left.

Use insteadI woke up, ate breakfast, and left.

A sequence of completed actions needs the simple past for all three, not the continuous for the first one.

AvoidIt snowed all day yesterday (ok, but continuous fits better for duration)

Use insteadIt was snowing all day yesterday.

Describing an activity that continued throughout a period often reads more naturally in the past continuous.

Topic 12 of 14

Simple Past I: Regular Verbs

Talk about completed past actions with regular verbs.

  • Regular verbs: base + -ed (walk → walked)
  • Verbs ending in -e: just add -d (live → lived)
  • Vowel + consonant: double the consonant (stop → stopped)
  • Consonant + y: change y to -ied (study → studied)
  • Negative/question: did + base verb (didn't walk, did you walk?)

AvoidI didn't walked to school.

Use insteadI didn't walk to school.

After “didn't,” the verb returns to its base form: walk, not walked.

AvoidDid you called her?

Use insteadDid you call her?

After “did” in a question, the verb stays in its base form: call, not called.

AvoidShe studyed all night.

Use insteadShe studied all night.

Consonant + y changes to -ied: studied, not studyed.

Topic 13 of 14

Simple Past II: Irregular Verbs

Master the past forms that don't follow the -ed rule.

  • Irregular verbs change form, not just add -ed
  • go → went, eat → ate, see → saw, have → had, do → did
  • Negatives/questions: did/didn't + base form, same as regular verbs
  • Only the affirmative form is irregular

AvoidI goed to the store.

Use insteadI went to the store.

“Go” is irregular: went, not goed.

AvoidShe eated dinner already.

Use insteadShe ate dinner already.

“Eat” is irregular: ate, not eated.

AvoidDid you went there?

Use insteadDid you go there?

After “did,” even irregular verbs return to the base form: go, not went.

Topic 14 of 14

Question Tags

Add a short question to confirm or check information.

  • Positive statement → negative tag
  • Negative statement → positive tag
  • The tag repeats the sentence's auxiliary verb (is, do, can, will…)
  • The tag always uses a pronoun, never the original noun

AvoidYou are tired, are you?

Use insteadYou are tired, aren't you?

A positive statement needs a negative tag, not another positive one.

AvoidShe doesn't like tea, doesn't she?

Use insteadShe doesn't like tea, does she?

A negative statement needs a positive tag.

AvoidMaria is late, isn't Maria?

Use insteadMaria is late, isn't she?

The tag always uses a pronoun, never repeats the noun.
