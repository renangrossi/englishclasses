What a Car Is Made Of

Engine, transmission, chassis, suspension, brakes — the parts a mechanic will name, and what each one does.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

A car has thousands of parts, but almost all of them belong to one of about six systems. If you know what those six do, you can follow most of what a mechanic tells you, and you can describe a problem in a way that gets it fixed.

The engine produces the power. In a petrol or diesel engine, fuel burns inside sealed cylinders, and the force of each small explosion pushes a piston down. The pistons turn a crankshaft, which converts that up-and-down movement into the rotation the car actually needs, and valves open and close to let fuel and air in and exhaust gases out. Timing is everything: the valves, the pistons and the ignition have to act within thousandths of a second of each other. An electric car does away with all of it. A battery supplies current to one or more motors, and the motors turn directly — far fewer moving parts, and almost no noise.

The transmission decides how that power reaches the wheels. An engine only produces useful power across a narrow range of speeds, so the transmission uses gears to keep it inside that range whether the car is pulling away from a stop or traveling at a hundred kilometers an hour. A low gear gives more turning force and less speed, which is what you want on a hill; a high gear gives the opposite. A manual transmission makes the driver choose; an automatic chooses for them. Electric motors produce their full turning force from a standstill, which is why most electric cars need only a single gear.

From the transmission, the power passes through the drivetrain — the shafts and the differential — to the wheels. The differential exists because of a problem that is easy to miss: when a car turns a corner, the outside wheel travels further than the inside one, so the two cannot be locked together at the same speed. The differential lets them turn at different rates. Cars send this power to the front wheels, the rear wheels, or all four, and all-wheel drive gives more grip on a loose or wet surface.

The chassis is the structure everything else is attached to — usually steel, sometimes aluminum, and designed as much to absorb the energy of a crash as to hold the car together. Mounted on it is the suspension: springs, dampers and the arms that hold the wheels in position. Its job is not only comfort. A wheel that bounces off the road surface is a wheel that is not steering or braking, so suspension is a safety system as much as a ride-quality one.

The wheels and tyres are the only parts of a car that touch the road, and every force — accelerating, braking, cornering — passes through four contact patches each about the size of a hand. Tyre condition therefore matters more than most drivers assume. Inside the wheels are the brakes: a disc that turns with the wheel, and a caliper that squeezes pads against it. The friction converts the car's movement into heat, which is why brakes get extremely hot and why they fade when used hard for too long.

Finally there is the cabin and its controls. The steering system turns the wheel's rotation into movement at the front wheels through a rack and pinion. The dashboard reports what the car is doing. Modern cars add a great deal more — screens, climate control, cameras, sensors that brake for you if you do not — but the underlying six systems are unchanged. The car still burns or stores energy, converts it to rotation, sends it to the wheels, holds itself together, stays in contact with the road, and stops.

Key Vocabulary

  • cylinder the sealed tube in an engine where fuel burns
  • piston the part pushed down inside a cylinder by burning fuel
  • crankshaft the shaft that turns up-and-down piston movement into rotation
  • valve a part that opens and closes to control the flow of gas or liquid
  • exhaust the waste gases produced by a burning engine
  • gear a toothed wheel used to change speed or turning force
  • turning force the strength with which something is twisted or rotated
  • standstill a complete stop
  • drivetrain the parts that carry power from the transmission to the wheels
  • grip the hold a tyre keeps on the road surface
  • chassis the structural frame everything else on a car is attached to
  • damper a part that absorbs and slows down a spring's movement
  • contact patch the small area of a tyre actually touching the road at any moment
  • caliper the part that squeezes the brake pads against the disc
  • to fade to lose effectiveness, said of brakes that have overheated
  • rack and pinion the gear arrangement that turns steering-wheel rotation into side-to-side movement

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The text says knowing the six systems lets you describe a problem so it gets fixed. Have you ever struggled to explain a fault to a specialist?
  • Electric cars remove most of the moving parts. What is gained, and is anything lost?
  • Which of the six systems would you least want to fail while driving, and why?
  • Do you think drivers should have to understand how a car works, or is that the mechanic's job?
  • Write about 120 words explaining how something you use every day works, naming its parts and what each one does.
