B1

Relative Clauses — Defining vs. Non-Defining, Whose & Prepositions

Add essential or extra information to a noun, and know when commas matter.

ObjectivesRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummary

Introduction

You already know who, which and that from A2. At B1, the key question is: does the relative clause tell you which one, or does it just add an extra fact about something already identified?

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Tell the difference between defining (essential) and non-defining (extra) relative clauses
  • Use commas correctly with non-defining clauses, and never with defining ones
  • Use whose to show possession
  • Use relative clauses with prepositions in both an informal and a formal style

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Defining relative clauses (essential information)

No commas. The clause tells you exactly which person or thing is meant — remove it, and the sentence loses essential meaning. That is allowed here (as well as who/which).

  • The woman who lives next door is a doctor. (Which woman? The one who lives next door.)
  • The book that I borrowed was excellent. (Which book? The one I borrowed.)

b) Non-defining relative clauses (extra information)

Commas on both sides. The clause adds extra, non-essential detail — remove it, and the sentence still makes complete sense. That is never used here — only who/which/whose.

  • My sister, who lives in London, is visiting next week. (I have one sister; the clause just adds a fact.)
  • The Eiffel Tower, which was built in 1889, attracts millions of visitors. (There's only one Eiffel Tower — the clause doesn't identify it, just adds information.)

c) Whose (possession)

Use whose to show that something belongs to the person or thing just mentioned — for people and things.

  • That's the man whose car was stolen.
  • It's a company whose products are sold worldwide.

d) Relative clauses with prepositions

Informal (everyday spoken English): preposition stays at the end, and that or nothing is common.

  • She's the woman (that) I told you about.

Formal (written English): preposition moves before whom/which — never before "who" or "that."

  • She's the woman about whom I told you.

Examples

See It in Action

  • The man who called this morning didn't leave his name.
  • My car, which is ten years old, still runs perfectly.
  • This is the restaurant that we talked about.
  • Mr. Silva, whose daughter studies with me, is a professor.
  • The company I work for is expanding to three new countries.
  • Paris, which is the capital of France, is famous for its museums.
  • The person to whom this letter is addressed no longer lives here.
  • The keys that you were looking for are on the table.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidMy sister that lives in London is visiting.

Use insteadMy sister, who lives in London, is visiting.

"That" is never used in a non-defining clause — use "who"/"which," with commas.

AvoidThe man, who called this morning, didn't leave his name.

Use insteadThe man who called this morning didn't leave his name.

This clause identifies which man — it's defining, so no commas.

AvoidThe woman who's car was stolen is my neighbor.

Use insteadThe woman whose car was stolen is my neighbor.

"Whose" shows possession; "who's" is short for "who is" — they sound the same but mean different things.

AvoidShe's the woman about that I told you.

Use insteadShe's the woman that I told you about. / She's the woman about whom I told you.

A preposition can go before "whom" (formal) or stay at the end with "that"/nothing (informal) — never directly before "that."

AvoidI have two sisters who live in London.

Use instead(only correct if you actually have more than one sister living in London — otherwise: My sister, who lives in London, is visiting.)

Whether you use commas changes the meaning: without commas, it implies you have several sisters and this identifies which ones.

Summary

Review

  • Defining clauses (no commas) tell you which one; that/who/which are all possible.
  • Non-defining clauses (with commas) add extra information; only who/which — never that.
  • Whose shows possession, for people and things.
  • A preposition can go before whom/which (formal) or stay at the end with that/nothing (informal) — never directly before that.
