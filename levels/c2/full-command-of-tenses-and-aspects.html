Introduction

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Habitual past in narrative — "used to" and "would" both describe repeated past actions, but "would" cannot describe past states (be, have, know, like) and is often preferred in literary narration for its rhythm.

Every summer, we would drive to the coast and would stay in my aunt's cottage. (not: we would have a cottage — states need "used to" or simple past: we used to have / had a cottage)

b) Iterative and habitual aspect — the present/past continuous with "always/constantly/forever" expresses a repeated, often irritating or characteristic, action, adding emotional color beyond a neutral simple tense.

She's always leaving her keys somewhere. (mild irritation/characteristic trait) vs. She always leaves her keys somewhere. (a neutral fact)

c) Tense-shifting for rhetorical effect — skilled writers and speakers deliberately break "expected" tense sequencing:

  • Historic present: switching to the present tense mid-narrative to make a past event feel vivid and immediate. "So I'm standing there, and suddenly the phone rings."
  • Future framed as inevitable/already decided: present continuous or simple present for scheduled/fated future events, adding certainty. "The results are announced next week." / "By this time next year, she retires."
  • Past perfect for retrospective narrative distance, even beyond its normal "earlier action" function, to create a reflective, looking-back tone in memoir-style writing. Precision here comes from choosing aspect (simple / continuous / perfect / perfect continuous) to signal not just when, but how the speaker frames an event — as a fact, a process, a completed result, or an ongoing/repeated experience.

Examples

See It in Action

  • Every winter, we would gather around the fire and tell stories until midnight.
  • He's always interrupting people — it's one of his more irritating habits.
  • So I'm walking down the street, minding my own business, when this car suddenly stops right in front of me.
  • By the time she turns thirty, she will have already published three novels.
  • The committee meets again on Thursday to finalize the budget.
  • I had lived in six countries by the time I turned twenty-five.
  • She used to hate coffee, but now she can't start her day without it.
  • He's forever losing his phone somewhere in the house.
  • Looking back, I had never really understood what my father sacrificed for us.
  • The train departs at platform four in ten minutes.
  • They had been arguing for hours before either of them said anything productive.
  • I'll have finished the report by the time you're back from lunch.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidWhen I was a child, I would have a dog named Max.

Use insteadWhen I was a child, I used to have / had a dog named Max.

"Would" cannot describe a past state (have, be, own, like) — only repeated actions.

AvoidShe always is losing her umbrella.

Use insteadShe's always losing her umbrella.

With "always" for emphasis/irritation, use the continuous form and place "always" between the auxiliary and the main verb.

AvoidYesterday, I am walking home when I see an old friend.

Use insteadYesterday, I was walking home when I saw an old friend. (or, for stylistic historic present, keep the whole narrative consistently in the present: I'm walking home when I see an old friend.)

Don't mix a past time marker ("yesterday") with the historic present without full commitment to the technique — it must be applied consistently, not just to one verb.

AvoidBy next year, she will finish her degree.

Use insteadBy next year, she will have finished her degree.

For a completed action by a future point, the future perfect is required, not the simple future.

AvoidI had never understand what he meant until years later.

Use insteadI had never understood what he meant until years later.

The past perfect requires the past participle ("understood"), not the base form.
