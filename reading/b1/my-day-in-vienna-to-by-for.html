A Day in Vienna

A friend arrives for the day — and the small prepositions that carry most of the meaning.

Yesterday was a good day. My friend Anna came to visit from Salzburg, and I had been looking forward to it for weeks.

She arrived by train at ten in the morning. She had booked the ticket herself on the ÖBB website, which is cheaper if you do it early, and she had sent me a photo of the confirmation the night before so that I would stop worrying about it. I waited for her on the platform. When she came through the barrier I hugged her, and we walked to the city centre on foot, because it was one of those clear October mornings when taking the underground would have felt like a waste.

We started with coffee at a small café near Stephansplatz. The barista was Italian, and he made the best cappuccino I have had in a long time — which, in a city that takes its coffee as seriously as Vienna does, is not a small thing to say. We sat by the window for nearly an hour.

Then we walked to the Hofburg, the old imperial palace, where there was a tour in English for visitors at midday. The guide was excellent. She had clearly given the same tour several thousand times and still seemed to find parts of it funny, which I always take as a good sign. We listened to her for an hour and a half and neither of us looked at a phone once.

In the afternoon Anna looked for a present for her mother and settled on a snow globe of the city, which she admitted was ridiculous and bought anyway. She paid for it in cash. We spent the rest of the afternoon walking, mostly without a destination.

We finished the day at a Heuriger on the edge of the city — a wine tavern where the wine is made by the family that owns it, and where you queue at a counter for cold food and carry it to a table in the garden. It was busy, we found a table anyway, and we stayed until it got cold.

Anna took the last train back. I walked home from the station, and the whole day had cost almost nothing, which was not the point but was pleasant all the same. It was a perfect day for both of us.

Vocabulary

  • to look forward to — to feel pleased about something that is going to happen
  • confirmation — a message proving a booking has been made
  • barrier — the gate passengers pass through at a station
  • on foot — walking, rather than using transport
  • the underground — a city's subway or metro system
  • barista — someone who makes coffee professionally
  • imperial — belonging to an emperor or empire
  • to settle on — to choose something finally, after considering others
  • snow globe — a small glass ball with a scene inside and artificial snow
  • ridiculous — silly; deserving to be laughed at
  • destination — the place you are travelling to
  • Heuriger — a Viennese wine tavern serving the wine made by its own family
  • tavern — a simple place serving drink and food
  • to queue — to wait in a line

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The writer says the day cost almost nothing, “which was not the point”. What makes a day out good, if not what you spend?
  • Anna bought a souvenir she thought was ridiculous. Why do people buy them anyway?
  • The guide had given the same tour thousands of times and still enjoyed it. How do people keep that up?
  • Describe a day you spent showing a visitor around your own city. Where did you take them first, and why?
  • Write about 120 words about a day with a friend, using to, by and for at least twice each.
