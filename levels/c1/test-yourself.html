Topic 1 of 10

Advanced Passive Structures

Report claims and describe obligations with passive reporting verbs and modals.

  • Passive reporting: subject + is said/believed/thought + to-infinitive
  • Passive with modals: modal + be + past participle
  • Get-passive: informal, often for accidents or change
  • Perfect passive infinitive: to have been + past participle

AvoidIt is said he is rich.

Use insteadHe is said to be rich.

The natural passive reporting pattern is subject + is said + to-infinitive.

AvoidThe bridge should finished by June.

Use insteadThe bridge should be finished by June.

Passive with a modal needs “be” before the past participle.

AvoidMy phone was got stolen.

Use insteadMy phone got stolen.

The get-passive already carries the passive meaning — no extra “was.”

Topic 2 of 10

Complex Subordinate Clauses

Link ideas precisely with concessive, purpose and result clauses.

  • Concessive: even though/although + clause; despite/in spite of + noun or -ing
  • Purpose: so as to / in order to + base verb; in order that + clause
  • Result: such + noun phrase + that; so + adjective + that
  • Whereas/while contrast two full clauses

AvoidDespite he was tired, he finished the race.

Use insteadDespite being tired, he finished the race.

“Despite” takes a noun or -ing form, not a full clause with a subject and verb.

AvoidShe studied hard so that to pass.

Use insteadShe studied hard so as to pass.

“So as to” or “in order to” + base verb express purpose; “so that to” is not correct.

AvoidIt was so a difficult exam that many failed.

Use insteadIt was such a difficult exam that many failed.

“Such” goes before a noun phrase (a difficult exam); “so” goes directly before an adjective alone.

Topic 3 of 10

Advanced Conditional Forms

Go beyond the basics with mixed conditionals, inversion and hypothetical structures.

  • Mixed conditionals combine a past condition with a present result, or the reverse
  • Conditional inversion drops “if”: Had I known…, Were she here…, Should you need…
  • “If it hadn't been for” / “but for” + noun = without
  • Formal, written English favors inversion over “if”

AvoidHad I know, I would have called.

Use insteadHad I known, I would have called.

Conditional inversion needs the past participle: known, not know.

AvoidIf it wasn't for you, I would have failed.

Use insteadIf it hadn't been for you, I would have failed.

This fixed expression always takes the past perfect form.

AvoidShould you needed help, call me.

Use insteadShould you need help, call me.

After “should” in conditional inversion, the base form follows, not the past tense.

Topic 4 of 10

Modality with Nuance

Speculate, deduce and criticize the past precisely with modal perfect forms.

  • Must have + past participle = strong deduction
  • Might/could have + past participle = a past possibility
  • Can't/couldn't have + past participle = strong deduction that something was impossible
  • Should have/shouldn't have + past participle = criticism or regret

AvoidHe must have leave already.

Use insteadHe must have left already.

Modal perfect needs the past participle, not the base form.

AvoidShe shouldn't said that.

Use insteadShe shouldn't have said that.

Criticism of a past action needs “have” between the modal and the past participle.

AvoidIt couldn't be her — she was with me all evening.

Use insteadIt couldn't have been her — she was with me all evening.

A deduction about the past needs the modal perfect, not the plain modal.

Topic 5 of 10

Inversion for Emphasis

Flip the word order after negative adverbials for a more formal, emphatic effect.

  • Negative/limiting adverbials at the start trigger inversion: Never, Rarely, Not until, Only after
  • “Not only” inverts the first clause; the second clause keeps normal order
  • “No sooner…than” and “Hardly…when/before” describe one event quickly following another
  • If no other auxiliary is present, add do/does/did

AvoidNot only he was late, but he also forgot the documents.

Use insteadNot only was he late, but he also forgot the documents.

“Not only” at the start of a clause triggers inversion of that clause.

AvoidNo sooner had I sat down when the phone rang.

Use insteadNo sooner had I sat down than the phone rang.

“No sooner” pairs with “than,” not “when.”

AvoidOnly by working hard you can succeed.

Use insteadOnly by working hard can you succeed.

“Only by…” at the start of a sentence triggers inversion of the main clause.

Topic 6 of 10

Complex Noun Phrases

Pack more meaning into a sentence with modifiers, nominalization and reduced relative clauses.

  • Multiple pre-modifiers stack before a noun: a rapidly growing economy
  • Nominalization turns a verb or adjective into a noun: decide → decision
  • Expanded relative clauses add extra, non-essential detail, set off by commas
  • Reduced relative clauses drop the pronoun and “be”: the man standing there

AvoidThe decision of increase prices was unpopular.

Use insteadThe decision to increase prices was unpopular.

A nominalized noun like “decision” is followed by a to-infinitive, not “of” + base verb.

AvoidThe report writing by the team was excellent.

Use insteadThe report written by the team was excellent.

A passive meaning (the report that was written) reduces to the past participle, not -ing.

AvoidA economy rapidly growing.

Use insteadA rapidly growing economy.

Modifiers stack before the noun: article + adverb + adjective + noun.

Topic 7 of 10

Reported Speech (Full System)

Report statements, questions and commands with the full range of reporting verb patterns.

  • Reporting verb patterns: deny + gerund, urge/warn sb + to-infinitive, insist on + gerund
  • Backshift in complex contexts: may → might, can → could, will → would
  • Reported questions use normal word order, with if/whether for yes-no questions
  • Modals would, could, might, should and must often stay the same

AvoidHe denied to have taken it.

Use insteadHe denied taking it.

“Deny” is followed by a gerund, not a to-infinitive.

AvoidShe warned me to not to be late.

Use insteadShe warned me not to be late.

Negative reported commands use “not to” + base verb, not “to not to.”

AvoidHe asked where was the station.

Use insteadHe asked where the station was.

Reported questions use normal (not inverted) word order.

Topic 8 of 10

Advanced Verb Patterns

Master verb + object + infinitive/gerund variations, perfect forms and causatives.

  • Verb + object + to-infinitive: persuade/allow/encourage sb to do
  • Verb + object + gerund: catch/keep sb doing
  • Perfect infinitive: seem/claim to have done
  • Causatives: have/get + object + past participle

AvoidThey persuaded him doing it.

Use insteadThey persuaded him to do it.

“Persuade sb” is followed by a to-infinitive, not a gerund.

AvoidI caught him to steal.

Use insteadI caught him stealing.

“Catch sb” is followed by a gerund, not a to-infinitive.

AvoidShe seems to have finish it.

Use insteadShe seems to have finished it.

A perfect infinitive needs “to have” + past participle.

Topic 9 of 10

Future Forms with Nuance

Handle future-in-the-past and complex future reference in narrative writing.

  • Future-in-the-past: was/were going to, would — a plan viewed from a past moment
  • Often used for plans that changed or never happened
  • “Will” backshifts to “would” inside reported/narrative contexts
  • “By the time” + future perfect describes a completed future action

AvoidI was go to call you, but I forgot.

Use insteadI was going to call you, but I forgot.

Future-in-the-past needs “going to,” with “to” included.

AvoidShe said she will come the next day.

Use insteadShe said she would come the next day.

“Will” backshifts to “would” in reported and narrative contexts.

AvoidBy the time you arrive, we will leave.

Use insteadBy the time you arrive, we will have left.

“By the time” + a future action needs the future perfect for a completed event.

Topic 10 of 10

Emphatic and Stylistic Structures

Emphasize exactly the part of a sentence that matters with cleft sentences and pseudo-clefts.

  • What-cleft: What + clause + is/was + the emphasized idea
  • Pseudo-cleft: The thing/person that… + is/was + the emphasized idea
  • It-cleft: It + is/was + the emphasized element + who/that + the rest
  • Used to highlight new, surprising or contrastive information

AvoidWhat I want it is peace.

Use insteadWhat I want is peace.

No extra “it” is needed after a what-clause.

AvoidThe thing that bothers me it is his tone.

Use insteadThe thing that bothers me is his tone.

The subject of a pseudo-cleft is the whole “thing that…” clause, with no extra “it.”

AvoidIt was Maria called, not Ana.

Use insteadIt was Maria who called, not Ana.

An it-cleft needs “who” (for people) or “that” before the rest of the clause.
