Introduction

The final C2 skill is the ability to rewrite entire passages — not just single sentences — while preserving meaning, tone, and logical structure, and to handle ambiguity and pragmatic nuance purely through grammatical choices.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Understand and use: Rewriting whole passages while preserving meaning — this requires holding the overall argument/narrative structure in mind while changing sentence boundaries, combining or splitting sentences, switching active/passive, and varying connectors, without losing or distorting the original content.
  • Understand and use: Handling ambiguity through grammar — deliberately using structures that preserve useful ambiguity (common in diplomacy, negotiation, and careful writing) or, conversely, eliminating unwanted ambiguity through precise grammar.
  • Understand and use: Pragmatic nuance through grammar — choices that signal social meaning beyond literal content: tentative language to soften criticism, passive voice to depersonalize blame, modal stacking to signal diplomatic uncertainty, or fronting to control what the listener focuses on first.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Rewriting whole passages while preserving meaning — this requires holding the overall argument/narrative structure in mind while changing sentence boundaries, combining or splitting sentences, switching active/passive, and varying connectors, without losing or distorting the original content.

Original: The company grew quickly. It hired hundreds of new staff. This led to problems with training.

Rewritten: As the company expanded rapidly, hiring hundreds of new staff in a short period, significant training problems inevitably followed.

b) Handling ambiguity through grammar — deliberately using structures that preserve useful ambiguity (common in diplomacy, negotiation, and careful writing) or, conversely, eliminating unwanted ambiguity through precise grammar.

  • Vague/deliberately non-committal: Mistakes were made. (passive with no agent — avoids assigning blame)
  • Precise/unambiguous: The finance team made several mistakes in the Q3 report. (active, agent specified)
  • Ambiguous modifier placement: Visiting relatives can be tiring. (unclear: relatives who visit you, or you visiting them?) → clarified: Relatives who come to visit can be tiring. / Going to visit relatives can be tiring.

c) Pragmatic nuance through grammar — choices that signal social meaning beyond literal content: tentative language to soften criticism, passive voice to depersonalize blame, modal stacking to signal diplomatic uncertainty, or fronting to control what the listener focuses on first.

Direct/blunt: You made a mistake in the report.

Diplomatically softened: There may have been a small oversight in the report — perhaps worth double-checking.

Near-native fluency means using these tools deliberately and invisibly: the reader/listener feels the tone and clarity you intend, without noticing the grammatical machinery behind it.

Examples

See It in Action

  • Original: "Sales dropped. We don't know why. We're investigating." → Rewritten: Sales have dropped for reasons that remain unclear, and the matter is currently under investigation.
  • Mistakes were made during the transition period. (deliberately vague, no blame assigned)
  • The finance team failed to reconcile the accounts before the deadline. (precise, agent specified)
  • There may have been some miscommunication regarding the deadline — it might be worth revisiting. (softened criticism)
  • Flying planes can be dangerous. (ambiguous: are the planes flying, or is someone flying them?)
  • Planes that are flying in bad weather can be dangerous. (clarified)
  • The proposal, though promising in theory, faced significant practical obstacles that ultimately proved insurmountable.
  • It is not that the plan was poorly designed; rather, external circumstances changed unexpectedly.
  • Whatever one's view of the decision, its consequences are now unavoidable.
  • The report could perhaps have been clearer in places, though its overall conclusions remain sound.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidSales dropped, we don't know why, we investigate it now.

Use insteadSales have dropped for reasons that remain unclear, and the matter is currently being investigated.

Combining short, disconnected clauses with commas (a comma splice) reads as informal and unclear at C2 — use proper connectors and consistent tense/aspect.

AvoidMistake was made during the transition.

Use insteadMistakes were made during the transition.

Subject-verb agreement — "mistakes" (plural) needs "were," not "was."

AvoidThere may had been some miscommunication.

Use insteadThere may have been some miscommunication.

After "may," always use "have," not "had."

AvoidFlying planes are dangerous when badly maintained. (still ambiguous if not intended)

Use instead[if referring to aircraft] Aircraft that are poorly maintained can be dangerous.

Leaving "flying planes" as a modifier keeps the structural ambiguity between "planes that fly" and "the act of flying planes" — clarify explicitly if precision matters.

AvoidIt is not the plan was poorly designed, rather circumstances changed.

Use insteadIt is not that the plan was poorly designed; rather, circumstances changed.

The "It is not that..." structure requires "that" to introduce the clause, and "rather" needs correct punctuation (semicolon or period) before it.
