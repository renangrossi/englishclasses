Saying It Again

What to say on a work call when you did not catch it, when you need to interrupt, and when the meeting has to be written down.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

On a video call in a second language, the hard part is rarely grammar. It is the audio, the speed, and the three or four seconds of silence in which you decide whether to admit that you missed something. Many people decide not to, and spend the rest of the meeting guessing.

The cost of guessing arrives later, and it is never small: the wrong date in the calendar, the wrong file, the work done twice. Asking costs about four seconds. The phrases below are the ones fluent colleagues use, and not one of them apologizes for anybody's English.

1. Asking for it again

Claire: — so we'll move the design review to the fourteenth, and Dan will own the migration.

You: Sorry, Claire, I didn't catch that — could you say it again? You're breaking up a little.

Claire: Of course. The design review moves to the fourteenth. Dan takes the migration.

You: Got it, thanks. And just to check — the fourteenth, not the fourth?

Claire: The fourteenth. One-four.

Two things happened there. The blame went to the line rather than to the listener, which is both polite and usually true. Then the number was read back. Dates, prices and names are exactly where a call goes wrong quietly, and reading them back costs nothing.

2. Interrupting without being rude

Dan: — and once the data is cleaned we can start on the dashboard, which should take about—

You: Dan, sorry to jump in. Can I check one thing before we move on?

Dan: Sure, go ahead.

You: When you say cleaned, do you mean the duplicates are gone, or that the whole validation pass is finished?

Dan: Good question. Duplicates only. The validation pass runs next week.

You: That changes my timeline, then. Thanks — carry on.

An interruption lands softly when it announces itself and then asks something specific. "Sorry to jump in" buys the floor; a precise question earns it. Compare that with staying silent and building a plan on the wrong meaning of one word.

3. The message that settles it

A call ends and everyone remembers it slightly differently. Whoever writes it down decides what was agreed, and that person may as well be you.

Hi Claire, Dan — short recap so nothing gets lost.

The design review moves to the fourteenth, not the fourth.

Dan owns the migration.

"Cleaned" means duplicates removed; the validation pass runs next week.

I'll send the revised timeline on Thursday.

Shout if I have any of that wrong. Thanks, both.

Six lines, no apology, no long introduction. It repeats the number that was corrected, it pins down the word that was ambiguous, it names who owns what, and it gives one deadline of its own. A message like that is read in fifteen seconds and prevents a week of confusion.

None of this is about sounding like a native speaker. It is about leaving the call holding the same information as everybody else on it — which is, in the end, the only thing the meeting was for.

Key Vocabulary

  • to catch something to hear and understand it — "I didn't catch that" is the normal way to say you missed a few words
  • to break up of a call: to arrive in pieces because the connection is poor
  • to read something back to repeat a number or name aloud so it can be confirmed
  • to jump in to interrupt a conversation
  • to buy the floor to win the right to speak for a moment
  • to carry on to continue
  • to move on to go to the next topic
  • a timeline the plan of what will be finished when
  • a recap a short repetition of the main points
  • to pin something down to make something vague exact enough to rely on
  • to own something at work: to be the person responsible for it
  • a duplicate a second copy of the same record
  • to shout here, in a message: to tell somebody informally — "shout if I'm wrong" means say so

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Role-play the first scene, but with a number that matters to you — a price, a flight time, a door code. Read it back.
  • Have you ever stayed quiet on a call instead of asking? What did it cost later?
  • The text says not to apologize for your English. Do you agree, and what would you say instead?
  • Write the recap message for your own last meeting, in six lines or fewer. Name who owns what, and give one deadline of your own.
