Topic 1 of 10

Mixed Conditionals

Combine two different time frames in a single conditional sentence.

  • Past condition → present result: If + past perfect, … would + base verb
  • Present condition → past result: If + simple past, … would have + past participle
  • Used when an unreal past event still affects the present, or an ongoing trait would have changed the past
  • Never use “would” inside the if-clause itself

AvoidIf she didn't miss the flight, she would be here now.

Use insteadIf she hadn't missed the flight, she would be here now.

The past condition needs the past perfect, even though the result is in the present.

AvoidIf he isn't so lazy, he wouldn't have failed the class.

Use insteadIf he weren't so lazy, he wouldn't have failed the class.

A present unreal condition takes the simple past (were), not the simple present.

AvoidIf I would have more time, I would have finished it.

Use insteadIf I had more time, I would have finished it.

“Would” never appears in the if-clause — only in the result clause.

Topic 2 of 10

Advanced Passive Voice

Use the get-passive, passives with modals, and passive reporting structures.

  • Get-passive: informal, often for accidents or change (My car got stolen.)
  • Passive with modals: modal + be + past participle (should be finished)
  • Passive reporting: subject + is said/believed/thought + to + infinitive (He is said to be rich.)
  • Passive infinitive after modals: The report needs to be revised.

AvoidThe window was got broken.

Use insteadThe window got broken.

The get-passive already carries the passive meaning — don't add “was” before “got.”

AvoidIt is said he is rich.

Use insteadHe is said to be rich.

The more natural passive reporting pattern is subject + is said + to-infinitive.

AvoidThe work should finished by Friday.

Use insteadThe work should be finished by Friday.

Passive with a modal needs “be” before the past participle.

Topic 3 of 10

Reported Speech (Full System)

Report statements, questions and commands accurately, with correct backshifting.

  • Backshift: present → past, past → past perfect, will → would
  • Reported yes/no questions use if/whether, with no auxiliary inversion
  • Reported wh-questions keep the wh-word, with no auxiliary inversion
  • Reported commands: verb + object + to-infinitive (told me to leave)
  • Time and place words shift: today → that day, here → there, tomorrow → the next day

AvoidShe said me that she was tired.

Use insteadShe told me that she was tired.

“Say” doesn't take a direct object — use “tell” when you mention who was told.

AvoidHe asked what did I want.

Use insteadHe asked what I wanted.

Reported questions use normal word order — no auxiliary inversion.

AvoidThey told to leave immediately.

Use insteadThey told us to leave immediately.

“Tell” always needs an object before the to-infinitive.

Topic 4 of 10

Future Perfect & Future Continuous

Talk about what will already be finished, and what will be in progress, at a point in the future.

  • Future perfect: will have + past participle — finished before a future point
  • Future continuous: will be + -ing — in progress at a future point
  • “By” + time usually signals the future perfect
  • “At” + time usually signals the future continuous

AvoidBy next year, I will finish the course.

Use insteadBy next year, I will have finished the course.

“By” + a future time point needs the future perfect, not the simple future.

AvoidThis time tomorrow, I will fly to Paris.

Use insteadThis time tomorrow, I will be flying to Paris.

An action in progress at a specific future moment needs the future continuous.

AvoidI will have been finishing the report by 5pm.

Use insteadI will have finished the report by 5pm.

A completed action by a deadline needs the simple future perfect, not the continuous form.

Topic 5 of 10

Modal Perfect

Speculate, deduce and criticize past actions with modal + have + past participle.

  • Must have + past participle = strong deduction about the past
  • Might/could have + past participle = a past possibility
  • Should have + past participle = criticism or regret about an unmet expectation
  • Shouldn't have + past participle = criticism of something that did happen
  • Can't/couldn't have + past participle = strong deduction that something was impossible

AvoidHe must has left already.

Use insteadHe must have left already.

After a modal, always use “have,” never “has” or “had.”

AvoidYou should went earlier.

Use insteadYou should have gone earlier.

Modal perfect needs “have” + past participle, not the simple past.

AvoidShe can't have forget the meeting.

Use insteadShe can't have forgotten the meeting.

The past participle of “forget” is “forgotten,” not “forget.”

Topic 6 of 10

Cleft Sentences

Emphasize the part of a sentence that matters most.

  • What-cleft: What + clause + is/was + the emphasized idea
  • It-cleft: It + is/was + the emphasized element + who/that + rest of the sentence
  • All-cleft: All + clause + is/was + the emphasized idea
  • Used to highlight new, surprising or contrastive information

AvoidWhat I need it is a break.

Use insteadWhat I need is a break.

No extra “it” is needed after a what-clause.

AvoidIt was John called, not Mary.

Use insteadIt was John who called, not Mary.

An it-cleft needs “who” or “that” before the rest of the clause.

AvoidWhat surprised me it was the price.

Use insteadWhat surprised me was the price.

The what-clause functions as the subject — no extra “it” before the verb.

Topic 7 of 10

Inversion for Emphasis

Flip the word order after negative adverbials for a more formal, emphatic effect.

  • Negative/limiting adverbials at the start trigger inversion: Never, Rarely, Not until, Only after, Little
  • Structure: adverbial + auxiliary + subject + main verb
  • If there is no auxiliary already, add do/does/did
  • Mostly used in formal, written or emphatic spoken English

AvoidNever I have seen such a mess.

Use insteadNever have I seen such a mess.

After a fronted negative adverbial, the auxiliary comes before the subject.

AvoidRarely we go out on weekdays.

Use insteadRarely do we go out on weekdays.

With no other auxiliary present, “do/does/did” must be added for the inversion.

AvoidOnly after I finished did I realized my mistake.

Use insteadOnly after I finished did I realize my mistake.

After “did,” the main verb stays in the base form: realize, not realized.

Topic 8 of 10

Reduced Relative Clauses

Shorten relative clauses using participles for tighter, more natural sentences.

  • Active reduction: who/which + be + -ing → -ing alone (the man who is standing → the man standing)
  • Passive reduction: who/which + be + past participle → past participle alone (the book which was written → the book written)
  • Only subject relative clauses can be reduced this way, not object relative clauses
  • Common in formal writing, reports and news headlines

AvoidThe report writing by the team was excellent.

Use insteadThe report written by the team was excellent.

The report doesn't write itself — the passive meaning needs the past participle “written.”

AvoidPeople living in the city, they complain about traffic.

Use insteadPeople living in the city complain about traffic.

The reduced clause replaces the relative pronoun entirely — no extra subject pronoun is needed.

AvoidThe car parking outside is mine.

Use insteadThe car parked outside is mine.

A car doesn't park itself — the passive meaning needs “parked,” not “parking.”

Topic 9 of 10

Advanced Connectors

Link and contrast ideas precisely with whereas, despite, furthermore and nevertheless.

  • Contrast between clauses: whereas, while (with a comma, joining two clauses)
  • Despite / in spite of + noun or -ing form, never directly + a clause
  • To contrast with a full clause: despite/in spite of the fact that + clause
  • Addition between sentences: furthermore, moreover, in addition (followed by a comma)
  • Concession between sentences: nevertheless, however (followed by a comma)

AvoidDespite the weather was bad, we went hiking.

Use insteadDespite the fact that the weather was bad, we went hiking.

“Despite” can't be followed directly by a clause — use a noun, -ing form, or “the fact that.”

AvoidIn spite it was expensive, we bought it.

Use insteadIn spite of the cost, we bought it.

“In spite of” needs “of” and is followed by a noun phrase, not a bare clause.

AvoidI like tea. Furthermore I like coffee.

Use insteadI like tea. Furthermore, I like coffee.

Sentence-initial connectors like “furthermore” and “however” are followed by a comma.

Topic 10 of 10

Wish / If Only

Express regret and unreal wishes about the present, the past, and other people's habits.

  • Wish + simple past = a regret about the present (I wish I were taller)
  • Wish + past perfect = a regret about the past (I wish I had studied)
  • Wish + would + base verb = annoyance about someone else's present habit or refusal to change
  • If only is a stronger, more emotional version of wish, with the same grammar patterns

AvoidI wish I am taller.

Use insteadI wish I were taller.

A present regret needs the simple past (were), not the simple present.

AvoidI wish I studied harder yesterday.

Use insteadI wish I had studied harder yesterday.

A past regret needs the past perfect, not the simple past.

AvoidI wish you would stopped interrupting me.

Use insteadI wish you would stop interrupting me.

After “wish… would,” the verb stays in the base form.
