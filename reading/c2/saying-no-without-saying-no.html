Saying No Without Saying No

Nobody in the meeting objected. The project was dead within a month. Both of those things are true, and the second one follows from the first.

The proposal went to the steering group in March. Nobody objected. One director said it was an interesting direction; another wondered aloud whether the timing was quite right; a third asked, pleasantly, what the appetite was elsewhere in the business. The minutes record no opposition, because none was expressed. The project was quietly dropped in April, and the person who had written it spent the summer wondering what had gone wrong in May.

Nothing had gone wrong in May. It had gone wrong in March, in a room where three people said no and one person did not hear it.

Professional English, and British professional English in particular, encodes refusal indirectly to a degree that is genuinely difficult for an outsider. This is not politeness in the ordinary sense, and it is certainly not weakness. It is a system, with a stable vocabulary and reliable rules, and it exists because a direct refusal in a meeting commits the refuser publicly, makes the refused party lose face, and forecloses the possibility that anyone will change their mind later without appearing to have lost. Indirection keeps all three of those doors open. The cost is that the message is carried almost entirely by register, and register is the last thing a learner acquires.

The phrasebook is small and worth memorizing. “I'd push back slightly on that” is not a slight objection; the hedge is conventional and the objection is usually total. “Let's park that for now” means the idea is dead, but nobody has to say so, and the speaker retains the option of resurrecting it if circumstances change. “I'm not sure that's the hill to die on” means drop it, and carries a faint warning that persisting will cost you something. “That's certainly one way to look at it” means it is the wrong way. “With respect” reliably introduces its opposite. “Interesting”, delivered flat, is not a compliment and was never intended as one. And the devastating “I hear you” acknowledges that sound has reached the speaker's ear while committing to precisely nothing beyond that.

What makes these hard is not that they are idioms. A learner can look up an idiom. It is that the surface meaning is not merely different from the intended meaning but the reverse of it, and that the markers which signal the inversion are the smallest words in the sentence: slightly, a bit, just, perhaps, quite, certainly. Strip the hedges out and the same sentences become ordinary. It is the hedge that tells a native listener to invert, and a learner who has been taught that hedges soften a statement will dutifully soften it and arrive at the opposite conclusion.

The geography matters too, and it is not the geography most people assume. Americans are markedly more direct than the British and routinely find British meetings maddening, but both are indirect by the standards of the Netherlands or Germany, where saying plainly that an idea is bad is a professional courtesy rather than an insult. Brazilians working across all of these tend to be caught twice: American bluntness reads as rudeness when it is merely efficiency, and British circumlocution reads as agreement when it is refusal. Neither reading is careless. Both are the predictable result of applying a sound instinct in the wrong room.

There is a further trap for the fluent. A speaker whose grammar and vocabulary are excellent is assumed by everyone present to have understood the register as well, so nobody repeats the message in plainer terms. The better your English, the less likely anyone is to tell you that you have just been refused. This is why high-level misunderstandings are so often invisible at the time and only surface weeks later, in the form of a project that stopped moving without anyone deciding to stop it.

Decoding is the necessary skill; adopting the style is optional and should be approached with care. Over-hedged English in the mouth of someone who has learned the forms but not the timing reads as evasive, or simply as weak, and it is better to be plainly direct than unconvincingly indirect. The useful habit is narrower than imitation. Listen for the hedges rather than through them. When you hear one, assume the strongest reading of the objection and ask a question that forces the position into the open — what would have to be true for this to work, or who else would need to be persuaded. Asked in good faith, that question is almost always answered honestly, because it offers the other person a way to say no that does not require them to say it.

The director who asked about appetite elsewhere in the business was telling the proposal's author exactly what was wrong and exactly who had to be won over. It was the most useful thing said in the room. It was heard as small talk.

Key Vocabulary

  • steering group a committee that decides the direction of a project
  • to wonder aloud to raise a doubt by appearing merely to think out loud — a way of objecting without owning the objection
  • appetite willingness to support or take on something, in business usage
  • ordinary normal, usual — careful: it is not an insult
  • to lose face to be publicly humiliated or diminished
  • to foreclose to shut off a possibility before it can be taken
  • indirection the practice of saying something by not saying it
  • hedge a word that weakens a statement on the surface — slightly, a bit, perhaps
  • to push back to object or resist — in practice far stronger than it sounds
  • to park something to set an idea aside, usually permanently, without formally rejecting it
  • the hill to die on the issue worth taking a serious risk over
  • with respect a formula that reliably introduces disagreement, not deference
  • to intend to plan to do something — careful: not to understand
  • devastating extremely damaging in effect
  • an idiom a fixed expression whose meaning is not literal — careful: not a language
  • to invert to turn into the opposite
  • bluntness the habit of speaking plainly without softening
  • predictable able to be expected, given what came before
  • evasive avoiding a direct answer
  • to win somebody over to persuade somebody who was not persuaded before
  • in good faith honestly, without a hidden motive
  • small talk light conversation about nothing important

  • Think of a meeting where you later realized the answer had been no all along. What were the actual words?
  • Is refusal direct or indirect in your own professional culture? What are its formulas?
  • The text argues you should decode this register but be careful about adopting it. Do you agree?
  • “The better your English, the less likely anyone is to tell you that you have just been refused.” Has this happened to you?
  • Write about 300 words on a misunderstanding that came from register rather than from vocabulary or grammar.
