Intelligence Pills

Drugs that might sharpen a healthy mind, and the question nobody has worked out how to answer.

For about twenty years, a version of the same prediction has circulated: that healthy adults and children will take drugs to sharpen their thinking, and that this will become as ordinary as drinking coffee. The prediction keeps being made, which is itself worth noticing, because it has not yet come true in the way it was meant.

What is true is narrower. Drugs that improve attention and concentration exist and are widely prescribed, mainly for conditions they were developed to treat — methylphenidate for attention disorders, modafinil for sleep disorders. Both are also used without a prescription by people who have neither condition, particularly students, and this is common enough that surveys of university populations regularly find it.

What those drugs do for a healthy person is less impressive than the word “smart” suggests. The reliable finding is that they help you keep working: attention holds for longer, tiredness matters less, a dull task stays possible. Effects on the things people actually mean by intelligence — reasoning, judgement, original thinking — are small, inconsistent between studies, and in some tasks negative, because a drug that makes you willing to keep going at one approach also makes you less likely to abandon it for a better one. The honest summary is that they buy you hours rather than insight, and that people who are already rested and focused have the least to gain.

The ethical argument is usually framed around fairness in exams, and by analogy with sport: if athletes are tested, why not students? The analogy is weaker than it looks. A drug that lets somebody revise for nine hours instead of five is not obviously different in kind from the coffee everybody is already drinking, or from a quiet room, a private tutor, or not having to work evenings — all of which are unequally distributed and none of which anybody proposes to test for.

That is the genuine difficulty. The line between a food supplement and an unfair advantage is not one anybody has managed to draw in a way that survives examples. Caffeine is a stimulant, sold in every corridor. Vitamins are taken specifically to improve memory. Sleep improves cognition more than any pill and is available only to some students. If the principle is that an advantage bought with money is unfair, exams were never fair; if the principle is that anything pharmacological is unfair, the coffee has to go too.

The risks are the part that gets least attention and probably deserves most. These are prescription drugs with real side effects — dependence, cardiovascular strain, disrupted sleep, which is particularly self-defeating when the point was to study — and long-term data on healthy young users barely exists, because that study has not been done. Somebody taking modafinil the night before an exam is running a small experiment on themselves with no control group.

None of which means the prediction is wrong. It means it has been presented backwards. The interesting question was never whether people would take these drugs, because some already do. It is what a society does when the advantage is real, modest, unequally available and impossible to detect.

Vocabulary

  • to circulate — to be passed around and repeated
  • to prescribe — to order a medicine for a patient officially
  • prescription — a doctor's written order for a medicine
  • survey — a study that asks a large group the same questions
  • reliable finding — a result that keeps appearing across different studies
  • dull — boring and requiring effort to continue
  • inconsistent — not in agreement from one case to the next
  • to abandon — to give something up and stop
  • insight — a sudden clear understanding of something
  • to be rested — to have had enough sleep
  • by analogy with — by comparing it to something similar
  • to revise — to study for an examination
  • unequally distributed — not shared out evenly
  • to draw a line — to set a limit between what is acceptable and what is not
  • stimulant — a substance that increases alertness
  • side effect — an unwanted result of taking a medicine
  • dependence — the state of needing a substance to function normally
  • self-defeating — producing the opposite of what was intended
  • control group — the people in a study who do not receive the treatment

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • If a drug buys you hours rather than insight, is taking it before an exam cheating?
  • The writer says exams were never fair, because a quiet room and a private tutor are advantages too. Does that argument justify anything, or does it prove too much?
  • Should universities test for these drugs? What would that cost, and what would it achieve?
  • “Running a small experiment on themselves with no control group.” How should someone decide about a risk when the data does not exist?
  • Write about 250 words arguing for or against allowing cognitive enhancers in examinations, and address the strongest objection to your own position.
