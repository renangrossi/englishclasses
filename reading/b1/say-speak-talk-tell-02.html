Buying the Laptop

A day of shopping for electronics, and four verbs that English refuses to let you swap.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

I had been putting off buying a new laptop for about a year, and last Saturday the old one finally made the decision for me by refusing to turn on. So I spent the day shopping, which I do not enjoy, and came home having spent more than I meant to, which is the usual outcome.

The first shop was the good one. I told the assistant what I actually needed — something that could handle video editing without sounding like it was about to take off — and she listened, which is rarer than it should be. She spoke about three machines in turn and was honest that the most expensive one was more than I needed. Then she said something that decided it: “This one will still be fast in four years. That one won't.” I said I would take it.

The second shop was less good. I had gone in to look at smart home devices, and the assistant talked at me for ten minutes about a security system I had already said I did not want. When I told him I was only interested in a thermostat, he told me the thermostat was on sale, which turned out to be true, so the visit was not wasted. I bought the thermostat and left.

After that I looked online, because I have learned that nobody in a shop is going to tell you that the same software is forty per cent cheaper on the manufacturer's own website. It was. I bought an office suite and, in a moment of weakness I am still thinking about, a smartwatch that had been reduced twice.

In the evening I called my friend Marcus, who knows more about this than I do, and told him what I had bought. He said the laptop was a good choice and asked why I had bought the watch. I said I did not entirely know. We talked for half an hour about whether any of it was necessary, which is a conversation we have had before and will have again.

The next morning the new laptop arrived and did in ninety seconds what the old one had stopped being able to do at all. I have not spoken to Marcus about the watch since.

Key Vocabulary

  • to put off to delay doing something
  • outcome the way something finally turns out
  • to handle to be capable of dealing with a demanding task
  • to take off to leave the ground, said of an aircraft
  • in turn one after another
  • to talk at someone to speak to someone without listening to them
  • on sale offered at a reduced price
  • manufacturer the company that makes a product
  • a moment of weakness a brief failure of self-control
  • to reduce to lower the price of something
  • office suite a set of programs for writing, spreadsheets and presentations

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The writer praises an assistant for talking him out of spending more. Has anyone ever done that for you?
  • What is the difference between someone talking TO you and talking AT you? Describe an example.
  • “A moment of weakness I am still thinking about.” What was yours?
  • Is it reasonable to check a shop's prices online while standing in the shop?
  • Write about 120 words about something you bought recently, using say, tell, talk and speak at least once each.
