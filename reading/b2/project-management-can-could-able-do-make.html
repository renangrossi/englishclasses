Clarifying Roles and Responsibilities

A project manager and a sales manager work out who can do what, and by when.

In project management and sales, clear communication and well-defined roles decide whether a project lands. That matters most under a tight deadline, with a client who cannot be kept waiting. Being able to say precisely what you can do now, what you could do later, and what you might be able to achieve given the right conditions is a professional skill in itself. So is knowing whether you do something or make something, because the choice quietly shapes how a responsibility is understood.

Emma, an experienced project manager, is overseeing the development of a new e-commerce platform for a major retail client. Her job is to coordinate across departments — sales, development, design and quality assurance. Mark, the sales manager, is the client's main point of contact, and he is usually the one negotiating timelines and deliverables.

At the start of the week, Emma called a meeting with Mark to go over the project's progress and head off any roadblocks.

Emma: Mark, thanks for joining. I wanted to check in about the client's latest feedback. Do you think you can reach out and clarify their priorities? There's some confusion right now about which features are non-negotiable.

Mark: Absolutely, I can handle that. In fact, I think I'll be able to get them to name their top three requirements. I've built up a good rapport with their product team.

Emma: That's great to hear. If you could confirm those priorities by Wednesday, it would let us close out the design phase. The team is hesitant to move forward without a clearer scope.

Mark: Understood. By the way, did the team make any changes to the initial wireframes?

Emma: They've started, but there's a lot more to do. The designers are focused on the homepage layout, and they'll need your input before we move on to the other sections.

Mark: Sure thing. I'll review the wireframes once they're ready. Also — the client asked whether we could deliver a prototype earlier than planned. Do you think we'd be able to accommodate that?

Emma: That depends on the scope. If they're open to a simplified version for the prototype, we can aim for an earlier delivery. If they expect every feature in it, we'd need more time, not less.

Mark: Got it. I'll propose the simplified version and see whether they agree. It's always a balancing act — making the client happy without overpromising.

Emma: Exactly. And speaking of balance, how is the sales report for the executive team? Were you able to make the updates we talked about last week?

Mark: Yes, I've started working your suggestions in. There's still some analysis to do, but I'll have it ready by Friday.

Emma: Perfect. Once it's done, we'll make sure the report brings out the client's strategic value. That's what will justify any extra resources we ask for.

Mark: Agreed. Let me know if there's anything else I can support you with.

Emma: Will do. Thanks, Mark. Let's regroup after your call with the client.

The exchange shows how collaboration actually works between these two roles. Emma has to keep tasks clearly defined and make sure the team can hit its deadlines without cutting into quality. Mark's side is negotiation and relationship-building, and keeping what the client wants inside what the team can realistically deliver. Neither of them promises anything they cannot hold to, which is why the project is still on schedule.

Vocabulary

  • roadblock — an obstacle that stops work from moving forward
  • to reach out — to make contact with someone, usually to ask or offer something
  • non-negotiable — not open to discussion or compromise
  • rapport — an easy, trusting working relationship
  • scope — the agreed boundary of what a project does and does not include
  • wireframe — a simple outline drawing of a page's layout, made before any design work
  • prototype — an early working version built to test an idea
  • balancing act — a situation where two competing demands must both be satisfied
  • to overpromise — to commit to more than you can actually deliver
  • quality assurance — the work of testing and checking that what is built meets its standard
  • to regroup — to meet again after separate work to compare notes and decide the next step
  • to close out — to finish a phase of work and formally end it

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • When you promise something at work, do you use the firm form or the cautious one? Which gets you into more trouble?
  • Emma answers a request with a condition rather than a yes or a no. Is that helpful or evasive?
  • Mark calls his job “a balancing act” between the client and the team. Whose side should he take when they conflict?
  • Describe a time a project went wrong because a role was not clearly defined.
  • Write about 150 words replying to a client who has asked for delivery two weeks early. Say what you can do, what you could do, and what you would need in return.
