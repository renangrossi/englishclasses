The Night Shift

A hospital at three in the morning is a different institution from the one the day staff know.

The handover is at seven in the evening and it takes eleven minutes. The day team speaks quickly, in the clipped half-sentences of people who want to go home, and then they go home, and the corridor empties, and the building becomes something else.

Marisol has worked nights for nine years. She did not choose them in any strong sense. The post was advertised, the differential was thirty per cent, and she had a daughter starting school who could not be left at six in the morning. The decision made itself, as most such decisions do, and then it became a life.

The first thing she will tell you is that the work is not slower at night, only differently shaped. Fewer patients arrive, but there are fewer people to receive them, and the specialists who can be summoned in four minutes at noon are at home asleep. A decision that would be referred upward during the day is simply made. Night staff are more autonomous than they are supposed to be, which is both the attraction and the thing that wakes them at four in the afternoon.

Around two o'clock the ward develops its particular quality. Patients who were stoical at eleven begin to ask questions they would not ask in daylight, and the questions are rarely medical. Marisol has learned that a request for water at two in the morning is sometimes a request for water, and is often a request for somebody to stand there for ninety seconds. She gives the ninety seconds when she has them. The arithmetic of the ward decides whether she has them.

Her own body has been arguing with her for nine years and is winning on points. The research is not reassuring: shift work is associated with elevated cardiovascular risk, with metabolic disruption, with a measurable deficit in reaction time toward the end of a run of nights. She knows this literature better than most of the day staff, because people who work nights tend to read about working nights. She also knows that the studies describe populations and not individuals, which is the consolation available, and that it is a thin one.

The social cost is harder to quantify and, she says, larger. Her daughter is nineteen now. Marisol missed a great many evenings in the middle of that, and the missing was not dramatic — no single catastrophe, just the steady absence of a person at the hour when a household talks. Friendships thinned by the same mechanism. The people who remained were the ones who would meet at odd hours or not mind a reply at three in the morning, and that selection, she notes, is not accidental: most of them also work nights.

There are compensations and she is precise about them. The commute is twenty minutes instead of an hour. She has never once queued in a bank. There is a camaraderie among people who are awake together while a city sleeps that she has not found anywhere else, and it is not nostalgia talking, because it is still happening. And there is the specific satisfaction of being the person who is there — not the best clinician in the building, which she is not, but the one present at the moment when presence is what is required.

At six the light changes in the windows along the east corridor and the building starts to refill. The day team arrives with coffee and weather and the loud competence of people who have slept. Marisol gives the handover, which takes eleven minutes, and the ward becomes somebody else's.

She drives home against the traffic. She is asleep by eight, and the city she lives in is one she mostly reads about.

Vocabulary

  • handover — the formal transfer of responsibility between shifts
  • clipped — short and abrupt, said of speech
  • differential — extra pay for working unsocial hours
  • to summon — to call somebody to come
  • to refer upward — to pass a decision to somebody more senior
  • autonomous — acting without needing permission
  • ward — a room or section of a hospital holding several patients
  • stoical — enduring difficulty without complaining
  • arithmetic — the plain counting of what there is — here, of time and staff
  • on points — winning narrowly over time rather than decisively
  • elevated — higher than normal
  • disruption — a breaking of the normal working order
  • deficit — a shortfall against what there should be
  • literature — the body of published research on a subject
  • consolation — something that makes a hard fact easier to bear
  • to quantify — to express as a number
  • catastrophe — a sudden great disaster
  • to thin — to become fewer or weaker
  • selection — the process by which some are kept and others fall away
  • compensation — something good that makes up for something bad
  • to queue — to wait in a line
  • camaraderie — warm trust between people sharing hard conditions
  • nostalgia — fond feeling for a past that is over
  • clinician — a medical professional who treats patients

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Have you ever worked unsocial hours? What did it cost and what did it pay?
  • “The decision made itself, as most such decisions do.” Is that true of a decision in your own life?
  • Marisol's remaining friends also work nights. What does that suggest about how friendships survive?
  • Should employers be allowed to staff hospitals this way, given the health evidence?
  • Write about 300 words describing a place you know at an hour most people never see it.
