B2

Paired Expressions

Two-part connectors for options, additions and opinions.

ObjectivesExplanationRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummary

Introduction

You already use and, but and or on their own. Paired expressions work in two halves, and each first half commits you to one specific second half — pick the wrong partner and the sentence breaks. This lesson drills the six pairs on the B2 sheet, plus the verb agreement rule that catches most students out.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Use either … or and neither … nor to offer and rule out options
  • Use both … and and not only … but also to add information with emphasis
  • Use rather … than and whether … or for preference and indifference
  • Choose the right verb form after a paired subject

Explanation

Explanation

A paired (or correlative) expression joins two elements with two linking words that always travel together: either … or, neither … nor, both … and, not only … but also, rather … than and whether … or. Two things matter: choosing the right partner word, and putting the same kind of element on each side — two nouns, two verbs or two clauses, never one of each.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Options: either … or / neither … nor

  • either … or presents two possibilities, one of which applies: You can have either the soup or the salad.
  • neither … nor is the negative version — it rules both out: I drink neither coffee nor tea.
  • nor is already negative, so the verb stays positive: Neither this nor that works (not doesn't work).

b) Addition: both … and / not only … but also

  • both … and adds two elements neutrally: Both you and I know the answer.
  • not only … but also adds a second, more striking point: I'd like not only the bread but also the cheese.
  • When not only opens the clause, the subject and auxiliary invert: Not only was she talented, but she was also kind.

c) Preference: rather … than

  • would rather … than states a preference, with a bare infinitive on both sides: I'd rather walk than wait for the bus.
  • No to and no -ing: not rather to walk than waiting.

d) Indifference: whether … or

  • whether … or says the outcome is the same either way: I'll go, whether it rains or not.
  • It can join phrases as well as clauses: Music is good, whether at home or in the car.
  • Only whether — not if — works directly before or not and before an infinitive: whether or not to accept.

e) Verb agreement after a paired subject

  • both … and is always plural: Both the cat and the dog want to go outside.
  • With either … or, neither … nor and not only … but also, the verb agrees with the nearer noun: Neither the kids nor the teacher is ready. / Neither the teacher nor the kids are ready.

Examples

See It in Action

  • You can have either the soup or the salad.
  • Neither this nor that option works for me.
  • Both you and I know the answer.
  • Not only was she talented, but she was also kind.
  • I'd rather walk than wait for the bus.
  • I'll go, whether it rains or not.
  • Neither the kids nor the teacher is ready.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidEither the bus or the train are fine.

Use insteadEither the bus or the train is fine.

After either … or the verb agrees with the nearer noun — here the singular "train".

AvoidBoth the manager and the staff was informed.

Use insteadBoth the manager and the staff were informed.

both … and always makes a plural subject, however singular each half looks.

AvoidNeither of us didn't know about it.

Use insteadNeither of us knew about it.

neither / nor is already negative — adding didn't makes a double negative.

AvoidI'd rather to stay home than going out.

Use insteadI'd rather stay home than go out.

rather … than takes a bare infinitive on both sides — no "to", no "-ing".

AvoidNot only she was late, but she also forgot the file.

Use insteadNot only was she late, but she also forgot the file.

When not only opens the clause, the subject and auxiliary invert.

AvoidI haven't decided if or not to accept.

Use insteadI haven't decided whether or not to accept.

Only whether can sit directly before "or not" and before an infinitive — "if" cannot.

Summary

Review

  • The six pairs: either … or, neither … nor, both … and, not only … but also, rather … than, whether … or.
  • Put the same kind of element on each side — two nouns, two verbs or two clauses, never a mix.
  • both … and is always plural; either … or, neither … nor and not only … but also agree with the nearer noun.
  • nor is already negative, rather … than takes bare infinitives, and not only at the start of a clause forces inversion.
