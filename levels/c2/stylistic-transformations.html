Introduction

At C2, you can re-express the same underlying idea in multiple grammatical forms, shifting tone and register without changing the core meaning. This is a key skill for editing your own writing and adapting to different audiences.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Active to passive rephrasing at high nuance — beyond the basic transformation, choose active or passive deliberately based on what should be emphasized, and how formal/impersonal the text should sound.

Active (direct, agent-focused): The board rejected the proposal.

Passive (formal, result-focused, agent optional): The proposal was rejected (by the board). / The proposal was rejected, a decision that surprised many. (agent omitted entirely — common in formal/academic writing when the actor is unknown or unimportant)

b) Multiple ways to re-express the same idea with tone changes — the same content can be delivered as a blunt statement, a hedged academic claim, a formal announcement, or a rhetorical question, depending on audience and purpose.

Blunt: The plan failed.

Hedged/academic: It could be argued that the plan did not achieve its intended outcomes.

Formal/official: The initiative did not meet its stated objectives.

Rhetorical: Can anyone really claim that this plan succeeded?

c) Grammar-driven style shifts — using specific grammatical choices to signal register:

  • Nominalization + passive = formal/academic/legal ("The implementation of the policy was delayed" vs. "They delayed implementing the policy").
  • Modal hedging (may, might, could, it would seem that) = cautious, academic tone.
  • Contractions, phrasal verbs, and direct imperatives = informal/conversational tone.
  • Cleft sentences and fronting = emphatic, persuasive, rhetorical tone. Mastering these transformations means you can take one idea and dial its formality, distance, and emphasis up or down at will.

Examples

See It in Action

  • The committee approved the budget. → The budget was approved (by the committee).
  • They messed up the schedule badly. → The scheduling was severely mismanaged.
  • I think the plan won't work. → It could be argued that the plan is unlikely to succeed.
  • We're cutting costs next quarter. → Cost reductions will be implemented in the next quarter.
  • Nobody knows exactly why the project failed. → The precise cause of the project's failure remains unclear.
  • He clearly lied to the board. → It would appear that the information presented to the board was not entirely accurate.
  • Fix this now! → This issue requires immediate attention.
  • The team is incredibly talented. → The team's talent is beyond question.
  • Sales dropped a lot last quarter. → A significant decline in sales was recorded last quarter.
  • Everyone agrees the policy failed. → There is broad consensus that the policy did not achieve its objectives.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidThe proposal was rejected by they.

Use insteadThe proposal was rejected by them.

After "by" in a passive sentence, use the object pronoun ("them"), not the subject pronoun.

AvoidIt could be argue that the plan failed.

Use insteadIt could be argued that the plan failed.

The passive hedge needs the past participle ("argued"), not the base form.

AvoidThe implementation of the policy delayed.

Use insteadThe implementation of the policy was delayed.

Nominalized subjects still need a full passive verb ("was delayed"), not just the past participle alone.

AvoidA significant decline of sales was recorded.

Use insteadA significant decline in sales was recorded.

"Decline" collocates with "in," not "of," when referring to a drop in a measurable quantity.

AvoidThere is broad consensus what the policy failed.

Use insteadThere is broad consensus that the policy failed.

"Consensus" is followed by "that + clause," not "what."
