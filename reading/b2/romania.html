Romania

Mountains, medieval towns and a Latin language in the middle of Slavic Europe.

Romania occupies the southeastern corner of Europe, and the first thing worth knowing about it is linguistic. Romanian is a Romance language — descended from Latin, and close enough to Italian that speakers of one can make a rough job of reading the other. That is unusual in a region where almost every neighbour speaks a Slavic or Hungarian tongue, and it is the direct legacy of the Roman conquest of Dacia in the second century.

The history since then has been a matter of absorbing whoever arrived next. The Dacians were a Thracian people; the Romans took the territory and left the language; and over the following centuries the lands that make up modern Romania passed under Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian influence in turn. The country as it exists now is recent by European standards, unified in 1918 out of provinces that had spent centuries under different rulers — which is part of why its regions still feel genuinely distinct from one another.

Geographically, the Carpathians run through the middle of the country in a long arc, and they are what keep Romania's wild places wild. The range holds some of the largest remaining populations of brown bear, wolf and lynx in Europe outside Russia, along with deep gorges and forest that has never been cleared. Hiking and skiing are the obvious draws. In the southeast, the Danube finishes its journey across the continent and breaks into the Danube Delta, a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the richest habitats for birds anywhere in Europe.

Transylvania, in the centre, is the region outsiders have usually heard of, and almost always for the wrong reason. Bram Stoker set part of Dracula there without ever having visited; Bran Castle, marketed ever since as “Dracula's castle”, has no real connection to the historical Vlad the Impaler, who was born in Sighișoara some distance away. What Transylvania does have is a genuine density of medieval towns — Sibiu, Brașov, Sighișoara — with their old centres intact, and the fortified churches built by Saxon settlers, which are a World Heritage site in their own right and considerably more interesting than the vampire industry.

Bucharest, the capital, is a harder city to like at first and a more rewarding one afterwards. Its nineteenth-century boulevards earned it the nickname “the Paris of the East”, and a good deal of that architecture survives between the concrete of the communist decades. The Palace of the Parliament, built under Ceaușescu, is among the largest and by some measures the heaviest buildings in the world; a whole district was demolished to make room for it, which is a fact the building's scale tends to obscure. The city also has serious museums, large parks and a nightlife that runs later than most of Europe's.

The culture is the same layered thing as the history. Folk traditions remain strong, particularly in the countryside of Maramureș and Bucovina, where wooden churches and painted monasteries have been in continuous use for centuries. Mărțișor, at the start of March, involves giving small red-and-white tokens to mark the arrival of spring, and Easter is the year's most elaborate celebration, with painted eggs and services that run through the night.

The food is substantial rather than delicate: sarmale, which are cabbage leaves rolled around seasoned meat and rice; mămăligă, a cornmeal porridge that does the work polenta does in Italy; and mici, small grilled rolls of minced meat that are the standard accompaniment to a beer outdoors. The wine industry is old and still underrated abroad — Fetească Neagră is the red most worth trying.

Romania has also produced a remarkable number of significant figures for a country of its size: the sculptor Constantin Brâncuși, who did as much as anyone to shape modern sculpture; the composer George Enescu; the gymnast Nadia Comăneci; and Herta Müller, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature for work largely about life under the Ceaușescu dictatorship.

For a country this varied, tourism is still comparatively light, which is much of the appeal. The painted monasteries of Bucovina, the Saxon villages of Transylvania, the delta and the mountains are all reachable and none of them is overrun. That will not last indefinitely.

Vocabulary

  • legacy — something inherited from an earlier period
  • to absorb — to take something in and make it part of yourself
  • province — a large administrative region of a country
  • distinct — clearly different from one another
  • arc — a curved line or shape
  • gorge — a narrow valley with steep rocky sides
  • habitat — the natural environment where a species lives
  • density — the degree to which things are packed into an area
  • fortified — strengthened with walls and towers against attack
  • boulevard — a wide city street, usually tree-lined
  • to demolish — to knock a building down deliberately
  • to obscure — to make something hard to notice
  • layered — made up of distinct levels laid one over another
  • substantial — large and filling; solid rather than light
  • underrated — not valued as highly as it deserves
  • dictatorship — rule by one person or party holding absolute power
  • overrun — spoiled by having far too many visitors

  • Bran Castle is marketed on a connection it does not have. Is that harmless, or does it cost a place something?
  • The writer says Romania's regions feel distinct because they were unified late. Is that true of anywhere you know?
  • “None of them is overrun. That will not last indefinitely.” Should a writer recommend a place that tourism might spoil?
  • Which would you rather visit — the mountains, the delta, or the medieval towns? Say why in three sentences.
