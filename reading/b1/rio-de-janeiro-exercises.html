Hiking in Rio de Janeiro

A city with the largest urban forest in the world, and four ways into it.

Rio de Janeiro is known for its beaches, its Carnival and its landmarks, and rather less for the fact that a substantial mountain range runs straight through the middle of it. You can be on a city bus and forty minutes later be in rainforest, which is not true of many places of this size.

The reason is Tijuca National Park, the largest urban forest in the world. What is less widely known is that most of it is not original growth. The area was cleared for coffee plantations in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and by the 1850s the deforestation had begun to threaten the city's own water supply. The response was to replant it, largely by hand, over several decades. The forest that now covers those hills is a deliberate nineteenth-century restoration project, and it worked.

Within the park the trails range from level walks suitable for anybody to genuine treks that take most of a day, and the flora and fauna are worth slowing down for — there are monkeys, toucans, and, if you are unusually fortunate, sloths that most visitors walk directly underneath without noticing.

Pedra da Gávea is the serious one. It is a vast block of rock above São Conrado, and the route to the summit involves a section of bare rock steep enough that many people hire a guide for it. It is not a walk and should not be attempted casually or alone. What you get in return is a view along the whole coast that is better than anything reachable by cable car, and the particular satisfaction of having earned it.

Sugarloaf Mountain is the opposite proposition. A cable car takes you up in two stages, the view at the top is a full circle of the city and the bay, and it is busy at sunset for good reason. There are also paths on the lower section for anyone who would rather walk one stage and ride the other, which is a reasonable compromise and considerably cheaper.

A few practical points that guidebooks tend to leave out. Start early: the heat by eleven is serious and the afternoon cloud often takes the view away entirely. Carry more water than you think you need. Ask locally about which trails are fine to walk alone, because the honest answer varies by trail and by day and people who live there will tell you straight. And check the weather for the two days before, not just the day itself — the rock on Gávea stays slippery long after the rain has stopped.

What stays with most people is the contrast. You climb for two hours through dense forest, hearing nothing but insects and your own breathing, come out on a ridge, and there is a city of six million people directly below with the Atlantic behind it.

Vocabulary

  • landmark — a well-known building or feature that identifies a place
  • range — a connected line of mountains
  • rainforest — dense forest in a region of heavy rainfall
  • urban — belonging to a city
  • original growth — forest that has never been cut down
  • trail — a marked route for walking
  • trek — a long and demanding walk
  • flora — the plant life of an area
  • fauna — the animal life of an area
  • summit — the highest point of a mountain
  • cable car — a cabin carried up a mountain on a suspended cable
  • compromise — a solution that gives each side part of what it wanted
  • ridge — a long narrow crest of high ground
  • slippery — difficult to stand or walk on without sliding

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Tijuca was replanted to protect a water supply and became a national park. Can you think of another practical decision that turned out to have a much larger benefit?
  • The writer says to ask locals rather than follow a general rule about safety. When is local knowledge better than a guidebook?
  • Would you rather earn a view over two hours or reach it by cable car in ten minutes? Be honest about why.
  • Is there a piece of nature inside a city you know? What would be lost if it were built on?
  • Write about 150 words describing a walk you have done, including one piece of practical advice a guidebook would not give.
