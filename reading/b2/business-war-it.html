Where Technology Comes From

Military money, commercial pressure, and the long argument about who computing is really built for.

A great deal of the technology now used for ordinary commercial purposes began with military funding, and the relationship runs in both directions. Satellite navigation was built by the United States Department of Defense and opened to civilian use afterwards. Jet engines, radar, and much of early computing were developed or accelerated under wartime pressure. The pattern is consistent enough that it is worth understanding properly rather than through the version usually told.

The internet is the standard example, and the standard account of it is wrong. ARPANET, its ancestor, was funded by the Advanced Research Projects Agency, part of the US defense establishment — so the military money is real. But it was not built to keep communications running through a nuclear attack. It was built so that a small number of extremely expensive computers at different universities could be shared by researchers who could not all be in the same building. The nuclear-survivability idea comes from separate work by Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation on distributed networks, which did influence the design of packet switching, but was not ARPANET's purpose. The distinction matters, because the popular version makes the internet sound like a weapon that escaped, when it was closer to a tool for sharing scarce equipment that turned out to have much wider uses.

Business has since become the main driver. Companies depend on technology to operate at all, let alone competitively: an online retailer is a logistics and payments system with a shopfront attached, and the software that manages its stock and its transactions is not a support function but the business itself. That dependence creates the exposure that follows.

It also runs the other way. Technology changes how wars are fought — nuclear weapons altered the entire logic of great-power conflict after 1945, and drones and network attacks have changed it again, more quietly. And wars change business: a conflict disrupts shipping, cuts off suppliers, moves the price of energy and food, and does so far from where the fighting is.

The commercial exposure is worth setting out plainly, because the methods are more ordinary than their reputation suggests. Data theft means gaining unauthorised access to information — customer records, payment details — which is then sold or used directly. Ransomware encrypts an organisation's own systems and demands payment for the key; hospitals and local governments have been among the worst affected, because they cannot simply stop operating while they rebuild. A denial-of-service attack overwhelms a site with traffic until it becomes unreachable, which is used both for extortion and for disruption. Malware is the general term for software installed to steal data or hold access. Insider incidents involve someone who already has legitimate access and uses it. And phishing tricks a person into handing over credentials or opening an attachment.

That last one deserves its prominence. The great majority of successful intrusions begin not with a technical defeat of a system but with a person being persuaded to do something — which is why security training matters as much as any firewall, and why the most common way in has stayed the same for decades.

The best-known illustration is Kevin Mitnick. He began breaking into systems as a teenager in the late 1970s and 1980s, and in 1988 he was arrested for intrusions into Digital Equipment Corporation's network, serving a year. In 1992, with a warrant issued for violating the terms of his release, he disappeared, and spent more than two years as a fugitive while continuing to access systems including those of a telephone company. He was arrested again in 1995, pleaded guilty, and served about five years in total, including a long stretch in solitary confinement that his supporters argued was disproportionate.

What makes the case instructive is not the scale of what he took, which was modest, but the method. Mitnick was a social engineer above all. He got what he wanted mostly by telephoning people and persuading them he was someone entitled to it — a colleague, a contractor, an engineer with a problem. After his release he worked as a security consultant and author until his death in 2023, and the argument he spent those years making is the one his own case demonstrates: that an organisation can buy every technical control available and still be opened by one convincing phone call, because the weakest component of any system is usually the person operating it.

Vocabulary

  • survivability — the capacity to keep functioning through an attack or disaster
  • packet switching — sending data in small separately routed pieces rather than over one fixed line
  • logistics — the organisation of moving and storing goods
  • to disrupt — to interrupt the normal running of something
  • unauthorised — without permission
  • ransomware — software that locks an organisation's data and demands payment to release it
  • to encrypt — to convert data into a form that cannot be read without a key
  • malware — any software written to damage a system or steal from it
  • insider — someone inside an organisation who already has legitimate access
  • phishing — tricking someone into revealing credentials or opening a harmful file
  • credentials — the username, password or token that proves who you are
  • firewall — a barrier that filters traffic entering or leaving a network
  • social engineering — manipulating people, rather than software, to obtain access
  • to run in both directions — to work as cause and effect each way
  • to set out — to explain something systematically, point by point
  • to cut off — to stop a supply reaching someone
  • shopfront — the public-facing part of a business

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The text corrects a widely repeated story about the internet's origins. Why do you think the wrong version spread so well?
  • “The weakest component of any system is usually the person operating it.” Is that fair to the people, or does it excuse bad design?
  • Should an organisation ever pay a ransom? Argue one side and address the strongest objection to it.
  • Mitnick went from prison to security consulting. Should someone convicted of intrusion be allowed to work in security afterwards?
  • Write about 150 words describing a phishing attempt you have received, or one you can imagine, and exactly what made or would make it convincing.
