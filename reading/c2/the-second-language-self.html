The Second Language Self

People report being funnier in one language and more honest in another. Some of that is real, and the reasons are not the romantic ones.

Ask anyone who lives in two languages and you will get some version of the same report. They are blunter in one and more careful in the other; funnier in the first language and, in the second, a person who does not quite make jokes. Many say they are a colder decision-maker in the language they learned later, and a few say, uneasily, that they prefer the person they are in it. The experience is near-universal and is usually explained badly, so it is worth separating what is real from what is flattering.

The weakest explanation is the one people reach for first: that each language carries a spirit, and that speaking Portuguese makes you warm in the way Portuguese is warm. This has the structure of an astrology column. It survives because it is unfalsifiable and pleasant, and because the alternative explanations are less charming.

The strongest findings are narrower and considerably stranger. People reason differently about risk in a foreign language. Presented with the same choice framed as lives saved or as lives lost, bilinguals show far less of the framing bias when the problem is put to them in their second language; they are, in a measurable sense, more rational, and they are more willing to accept a small loss for a good expected outcome. The explanation is not that the second language is wiser. It is that it is quieter. Emotional weight accumulates on the words you acquired as a child, and reasoning conducted at a slight remove from that weight is less distorted by it — and also less informed by it, which is the part usually left out.

The same mechanism explains the moral findings, which are genuinely uncomfortable. Asked whether they would push one person to their death to save five, people say yes substantially more often in a foreign language. The ordinary gloss is that the second language strips away squeamishness and leaves the arithmetic. That is one reading. The other is that moral intuition is not noise to be filtered out but information, and a person reasoning in a language where that intuition is muted is not thinking more clearly — they are thinking with part of the apparatus switched off.

Then there is the ordinary and much larger effect, which needs no laboratory. Most people's second language is unevenly distributed across the parts of their life. It was learned at school and used at work, so it is excellent on budgets and thin on grief; it has a complete vocabulary for a quarterly review and three words for what you felt when your father was ill. A person who seems less emotionally present in their second language may simply have no instrument there. That is not personality. It is coverage, and it closes with use.

Register compounds it. A speaker working at the edge of their fluency spends capacity on construction, and the first thing sacrificed is tone. Irony needs a reliable baseline to depart from; warmth needs small talk that is not being assembled in real time. The result is a person who sounds flatter and more formal than they are, is heard as cold or arrogant, and knows it, which makes the next sentence worse. The feedback loop is the cruelty of the thing — and it is read, by nearly everybody involved, as character rather than as load.

The practical consequences are not small. Decisions get made in the language of the meeting, which means they are made by people operating under uneven handicaps and uneven emotional engagement. A team debating in English, where half the room is native, is not simply a team debating in English: it is one where some people have access to irony, hesitation and the softening clause, and others are spending their attention on the subjunctive. The quieter half is not less convinced. It is more expensive for them to object.

None of which supports the usual conclusion, that you have a true self in your first language and a performance in the second. The evidence does not show a real self and a mask. It shows one person with two unevenly equipped instruments, each permitting some things and obstructing others, and a quantity of emotional loading that is a fact about when words were learned rather than about who the speaker is. What people call becoming a different person in English is mostly the experience of thinking without the usual accompaniment — and the right response is neither to romanticize it nor to distrust it, but to notice which decisions you are making in which language, and to be suspicious of the ones that came too easily.

Vocabulary

  • blunt — direct to the point of being rough
  • uneasily — with discomfort
  • flattering — making somebody feel better about themselves than is warranted
  • unfalsifiable — impossible to prove wrong, and therefore of little explanatory use
  • to frame — to present a choice in terms that steer the answer
  • bias — a systematic distortion in judgment
  • at a remove — at a distance from something
  • to distort — to twist out of its true shape
  • gloss — the usual quick interpretation put on something
  • squeamishness — discomfort at something unpleasant or morally hard
  • intuition — a judgment arrived at without conscious reasoning
  • to mute — to reduce in volume or force
  • apparatus — the whole set of equipment a thing works with
  • unevenly — not equally across the whole
  • coverage — how much of a field your vocabulary actually reaches
  • to compound — to make an existing problem worse
  • capacity — the mental resource available at one time
  • baseline — the normal level that departures are measured against
  • feedback loop — a cycle where the result feeds back and strengthens the cause
  • load — the amount of effort something demands
  • handicap — a disadvantage imposed on some and not others
  • to romanticize — to treat as more beautiful or meaningful than it is

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Are you a different person in English? Describe the difference without reaching for the romantic explanation.
  • Where is your English vocabulary thin — which parts of your life has it never covered?
  • “It is more expensive for them to object.” Have you stayed quiet in a meeting for that reason?
  • Does the moral finding trouble you? Should it?
  • Write about 300 words on a decision you made in English that you suspect you would have made differently in Portuguese.
