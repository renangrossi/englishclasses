Complex Ellipsis and Substitution

Introduction

Ellipsis (omitting words that are recoverable from context) and substitution (replacing repeated words with a short pro-form) make advanced English tighter and more natural, avoiding clumsy repetition.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Understand and use: Ellipsis in reduced clauses — omitting a repeated subject, auxiliary, or verb when the meaning stays clear.
  • Understand and use: Omission patterns common in academic and literary writing — dropping relative pronouns and "that" in defining clauses, and omitting "which is/who is" before a reduced participle or adjective phrase.
  • Understand and use: Pro-forms in complex texts — using "do so," "one," "the same," "such," or simple auxiliaries to stand in for a whole verb phrase or idea already mentioned, avoiding repetition across sentences.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

a) Ellipsis in reduced clauses — omitting a repeated subject, auxiliary, or verb when the meaning stays clear.

  • After "and/but/or" in parallel structures: She ordered coffee and [she ordered] a croissant.
  • In comparative/contrastive clauses: He works harder than she [works]. / Some like tea; others [like] coffee.
  • After auxiliaries, when the main verb is recoverable: He hasn't finished yet, but he will [finish] soon.
  • In subordinate clauses with the same subject as the main clause: While [he was] waiting, he read the newspaper.

b) Omission patterns common in academic and literary writing — dropping relative pronouns and "that" in defining clauses, and omitting "which is/who is" before a reduced participle or adjective phrase.

The book [that] I mentioned is finally out. / The results, [which were] published last year, remain controversial.

c) Pro-forms in complex texts — using "do so," "one," "the same," "such," or simple auxiliaries to stand in for a whole verb phrase or idea already mentioned, avoiding repetition across sentences.

He asked her to leave, and she did so immediately. (= she left immediately)

If you need a pen, take one from my desk. (= a pen, not a specific one already mentioned)

The results confirmed the hypothesis; subsequent studies have done the same.

These devices are essential for sounding genuinely native-like: overusing full repetition, even when grammatically correct, sounds stilted at C2.

Examples

See It in Action

  • She likes jazz, and her brother does too.
  • He hasn't called yet, but he probably will soon.
  • Some prefer working from home; others don't.
  • While waiting for the bus, she finished her book.
  • The report [that] we discussed yesterday has been revised.
  • The proposal, [which was] submitted last month, is still under review.
  • He asked me to stay quiet, and I did so, reluctantly.
  • If you need an umbrella, take one from the stand by the door.
  • The first study confirmed the theory; the second one did the same.
  • She can speak French fluently, and so can her sister.
  • He wanted to leave early, but couldn't.
  • Whatever the outcome, we'll accept it.

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidShe likes jazz, and her brother does it too.

Use insteadShe likes jazz, and her brother does too. (or: ...and so does her brother.)

"Does too" alone functions as the substitute verb phrase — adding "it" creates redundancy.

AvoidHe hasn't called yet, but he will do it soon.

Use insteadHe hasn't called yet, but he will soon. / ...but he will do so soon.

Use the bare auxiliary or "do so," not "will do it," which is redundant/informal here.

AvoidWhile she was waiting, was reading a magazine.

Use insteadWhile waiting, she was reading a magazine. / While she was waiting, she was reading a magazine.

If you omit the subject after "while," you must also omit "was" (ellipsis applies to subject + auxiliary together); otherwise, keep both.

AvoidIf you need a pen, take the one from my desk. (when no specific pen has been mentioned)

Use insteadIf you need a pen, take one from my desk.

"One" (indefinite substitution) is used for an unspecified item; "the one" implies a specific, previously identified item.

AvoidHe asked her to apologize, and she did so it.

Use insteadHe asked her to apologize, and she did so.

"Did so" already replaces the whole verb phrase — don't add another object after it.
