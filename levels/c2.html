C2 is the summit of the course: full command of tense and aspect, highly complex sentence architecture, stylistic transformation across registers, and near-native control of modality and discourse.

C2

Speaking

Five near-native tasks: shifting register mid-conversation, leading and steering a discussion, and speaking precisely about ambiguity and implication rather than around it. At C2, the goal is no longer to be understood — it's to control exactly the effect your words have.

1. Subtle Register Shifts, Mid-Conversation

Deliver the same short story three times out loud, shifting register each time without changing the facts: first to a close friend, then to a room of academics, then to a journalist asking pointed questions. Notice which words, structures and even pacing change.

  • Recount a project at work that went badly wrong.
  • Describe a disagreement you witnessed or took part in.

2. Leading a Discussion

Practice opening, steering and closing a group discussion — even alone, speak all the parts: the facilitator's framing, a redirect when the conversation drifts, and a closing synthesis that fairly represents disagreement.

  • Facilitate a discussion on whether creative work produced by AI can be considered “authentic.”
  • Facilitate a discussion on whether privacy is a right that should ever yield to public safety.

3. Speaking About Ambiguity Without Resolving It

Discuss each situation out loud for one to two minutes, deliberately preserving the ambiguity rather than forcing a false certainty — say clearly what is unclear, and why.

  • A colleague's email ends with “Sure, that sounds fine.” Is it sincere or reluctant? Discuss what makes it genuinely ambiguous.
  • A public figure's statement could be read as either an apology or a justification. Discuss both readings without declaring one “correct.”

4. Reading Between the Lines: Implication

Say each statement out loud exactly as written, then say out loud what it implies without stating directly — the unspoken subtext a native listener would pick up on.

  • “That's certainly one way to approach it.”
  • “I wasn't aware we were still doing it that way.”
  • “You've clearly put a lot of effort into this.”

5. Impromptu Response Under Pressure

Without preparing in advance, respond out loud to an unexpected, sharp question for 60–90 seconds, structuring your answer on the spot — buy yourself thinking time with natural fillers rather than silence, then answer with precision.

  • “Some would say your position is simply outdated. How would you respond?”
  • “If you had to argue against everything you just said, what's the strongest counterargument?”

Revision

A quick reference grid, the most common C2 mistakes, and two mixed-review exercises pulling together the ten C2 focus areas.

Quick Reference: Key Structures

StructureFormExample
Advanced Discourse Connectorsnotwithstanding / nevertheless / moreover + clauseNotwithstanding these concerns, the plan proceeded.
Ellipsisomitting a repeated verb or phraseShe can play the piano, and he the violin.
Literary Inversionfronted adverbial/complement + inverted verb-subject orderGone were the days of easy certainty.
Layered Modalitymight have been expected to + infinitiveHe might have been expected to know better.
Emphatic Do (Literary)do/does/did + base verb, for emphasisShe did know, after all — she simply said nothing.
Formal Subjunctivethat + subject + base verb (be, for “to be”)It is essential that he be present.
Register Shiftingvocabulary and structure change with audience/context“Can't make it” (informal) vs. “I regret that I am unable to attend” (formal)
Near-Native Rephrasingrestating an idea while preserving meaning and toneThe proposal was rejected. → The board declined to move forward with the proposal.

Common Mistakes at C2

AvoidUse InsteadWhy
Notwithstanding of these concerns, we proceeded.Notwithstanding these concerns, we proceeded.“Notwithstanding” is followed directly by a noun phrase, with no “of.”
She can play piano, and he can plays violin.She can play piano, and he violin.Ellipsis drops the repeated modal and verb entirely, not just conjugates it differently.
It is essential that he is present.It is essential that he be present.Formal subjunctive after expressions like “it is essential that” uses the base form “be,” not “is.”
Gone the days were of easy answers.Gone were the days of easy answers.Literary inversion requires verb-subject order directly after the fronted element.
He might have expected to have known better.He might have been expected to know better.Layered modality needs the passive “been expected” before the plain infinitive “to know.”
