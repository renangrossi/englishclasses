Imperatives (Review and Expand)

A mixed review that puts your A1 tenses to work in one story.

ObjectivesExplanationRulesExamplesCommon MistakesPracticeSummaryRelated

Introduction

This review lesson combines the tenses you've learned so far — present simple, past simple, future simple, present continuous, and imperatives — inside one connected story, the way real English actually mixes them.

By the end of this lesson you can…

  • Choose the correct tense based on context clues in a story
  • Recognize imperatives inside natural dialogue
  • Review present, past and future forms together

Explanation

Explanation

Real English rarely uses just one tense at a time. A short story about a trip might use the past simple for what already happened, the present continuous for something happening at that moment in the story, and imperatives inside quoted dialogue (“Wake up early tomorrow,” Emily told James).

This lesson is a chance to practice recognizing which tense fits a given context, rather than learning a brand new rule.

Grammar Rules

The Rules

  • Past simple → completed actions in the story (decided, planned, arrived)
  • Present continuous → something happening at that moment (are visiting)
  • Future simple → plans mentioned in dialogue (we will go)
  • Imperatives → direct instructions inside quoted speech (“Pack your bags.”)

Examples

See It in Action

  • Last summer, Emily and James decided to take a trip to Europe.
  • “Wake up early tomorrow,” Emily told James.
  • They are visiting many historical sites this week.
  • “We will go to see the Tower of London,” she said.
  • “Don't forget to write about this in your travel diary.”

Common Mistakes

Watch Out For

AvoidLast summer, they decide to go.

Use insteadLast summer, they decided to go.

A completed past action needs the past simple: decided.

AvoidWake up early tomorrow (as a statement to yourself)

Use insteadWake up early tomorrow.

Imperatives use the base verb with no subject — this is already correct as a command.

AvoidThey visit many sites right now.

Use insteadThey are visiting many sites right now.

“Right now” signals the present continuous, not the simple present.

Tip

When you read a mixed story, underline the time clues (“last summer,” “right now,” “tomorrow”) — they usually tell you which tense to use.

Summary

Review
