Topic 1 of 10

Full Command of All Tenses and Aspects

Master precise, subtle tense contrasts and shift tense deliberately for rhetorical effect.

  • Habitual past in narrative: would/used to + base verb for repeated past actions
  • Iterative aspect: keep/keep on + gerund for repeated actions
  • The historic present narrates past events as if happening now, for dramatic effect
  • Tense shifts can signal changes in psychological distance or emphasis

AvoidEvery summer, we would went to the lake.

Use insteadEvery summer, we would go to the lake.

“Would” for habitual past is followed by the base verb, not the past tense.

AvoidHe keeps to interrupt me.

Use insteadHe keeps interrupting me.

“Keep” for repeated action is followed by a gerund, not a to-infinitive.

AvoidYesterday I go to the store and I see an old friend.

Use insteadYesterday I went to the store and I saw an old friend.

The historic present is a deliberate stylistic choice, not a substitute for the past tense with a past time marker like “yesterday.”

Topic 2 of 10

Highly Complex Sentence Architecture

Build long sentences with nested subordinate clauses, multi-clause fronting and dense noun phrases.

  • Multiple subordinate clauses can nest within a single sentence
  • Fronting a subordinate clause shifts emphasis toward it
  • Dense noun phrases stack modifiers before the head noun
  • Commas, dashes and semicolons manage complexity for the reader

AvoidHaving finished the report, the meeting began.

Use insteadHaving finished the report, she began the meeting.

A fronted participle clause must refer to the subject of the main clause — the meeting didn't finish the report.

AvoidThe report, that was written by the team, was late.

Use insteadThe report, which was written by the team, was late.

Non-restrictive (comma-separated) relative clauses use “which,” not “that.”

AvoidAlthough expensive, and even though the reviews were mixed, and while I had doubts, I bought it anyway.

Use insteadAlthough it was expensive and the reviews were mixed, I bought it anyway.

Stacking multiple concessive connectors for one idea is redundant — one clear subordinator is stronger.

Topic 3 of 10

Advanced Discourse Devices

Connect ideas across sentences and paragraphs with formal connectors and cohesive devices.

  • Notwithstanding + noun phrase = despite
  • Moreover/furthermore add a supporting point in formal writing, followed by a comma
  • “The former” and “the latter” refer back to the first and second of two things just mentioned
  • Words like “this,” “such” and “the latter” avoid repeating a whole idea

AvoidNotwithstanding of the risks, they proceeded.

Use insteadNotwithstanding the risks, they proceeded.

“Notwithstanding” is followed directly by a noun phrase, with no “of.”

AvoidThe results were promising. Moreover the funding increased.

Use insteadThe results were promising. Moreover, the funding increased.

Sentence-initial connectors like “moreover” are followed by a comma.

AvoidWe discussed two options; the latter, the first, was rejected.

Use insteadWe discussed two options; the former was rejected.

“The former” refers to the first of two items, and “the latter” to the second — they are not interchangeable.

Topic 4 of 10

Stylistic Transformations

Re-express the same idea multiple ways, shifting tone and register through grammar alone.

  • Active → passive shifts emphasis and can raise formality
  • Nominalization (decide → decision) raises formality
  • The same idea can be rephrased across registers without changing its core meaning
  • Word choice signals stance even when the underlying facts stay the same

AvoidThe company was very bad managed.

Use insteadThe company was poorly managed.

The adverb form “poorly” is needed before a past participle, not “very bad.”

AvoidIt was rejected the plan by the board.

Use insteadThe plan was rejected by the board.

The passive subject (“the plan”) must come before the passive verb, not after.

AvoidWe regret to inform you that we're totally screwed.

Use insteadWe regret to inform you that the outcome was unfavorable.

Mixing a formal opening with casual slang breaks register consistency.

Topic 5 of 10

Complex Ellipsis and Substitution

Omit and substitute repeated elements the way advanced native writing naturally does.

  • Ellipsis omits a repeated verb or phrase already understood from context
  • “So” can substitute a whole clause: I think so. I hope so.
  • An auxiliary alone can stand in for a full verb phrase: She can, and so can I.
  • After “so”/“neither” for agreement, the subject and auxiliary invert

AvoidShe likes coffee and he likes it too tea.

Use insteadShe likes coffee, and he tea.

The repeated verb “likes” is omitted through ellipsis once the meaning is clear.

AvoidI hope so that it works.

Use insteadI hope so.

“So” already substitutes the whole clause — no further clause is needed after it.

AvoidHe passed the exam, and so I did.

Use insteadHe passed the exam, and so did I.

After “so” for agreement, the auxiliary and subject invert: so did I.

Topic 6 of 10

High-Level Clefts and Inversions

Use literary inversion and layered pseudo-clefts for maximum stylistic impact.

  • Literary inversion: fronted complement/adverbial + inverted verb-subject order
  • Pseudo-clefts can stack across a passage for rhetorical build-up
  • Fronted modifiers place emphasis at the very start of a sentence
  • These forms are stylistic choices, reserved for formal or literary register

AvoidGone the days were of easy answers.

Use insteadGone were the days of easy answers.

Literary inversion requires verb-subject order directly after the fronted complement.

AvoidSuch the silence was that I could hear my own heartbeat.

Use insteadSuch was the silence that I could hear my own heartbeat.

Fronted “such” is followed directly by “was,” then the subject.

AvoidNever before had happened such a thing.

Use insteadNever before had such a thing happened.

After the inverted auxiliary “had,” the subject comes before the main verb.

Topic 7 of 10

Native-Like Use of Modality and Attitude

Shade meaning with subtle modality — tentativeness, concession, and layered modal structures.

  • Layered modality: might have been expected to + infinitive
  • Modal shading: might (tentative), may well (concessive but confident), would you mind (polite)
  • Epistemic modals show how certain the speaker is about a claim
  • Modals can combine with the passive and perfect for extra nuance

AvoidHe might have expected to have known better.

Use insteadHe might have been expected to know better.

Layered modality needs the passive “been expected” before the plain infinitive “to know.”

AvoidWould you mind to open the window?

Use insteadWould you mind opening the window?

“Mind” is followed by a gerund, not a to-infinitive.

AvoidShe may well have been right, but she may also have not been.

Use insteadShe may well have been right, though she may equally have been wrong.

A clean parallel structure avoids the clumsy double negative construction.

Topic 8 of 10

Rare or Literary Structures

Recognize and use the formal subjunctive, emphatic do, and inverted conditionals with an omitted auxiliary.

  • Formal subjunctive: that + subject + base verb (be for “to be”)
  • Emphatic “do/does/did” + base verb stresses the truth of a statement
  • Very formal writing can invert a conditional and omit “if” entirely: Were it not for…
  • These structures are rare in speech, more common in formal or literary writing

AvoidIt is essential that he is present.

Use insteadIt is essential that he be present.

The formal subjunctive after “it is essential that” uses the base form “be,” not “is.”

AvoidShe really liked it, in fact.

Use insteadShe did like it, in fact.

Emphatic “did” + base verb stresses the truth of the statement more strongly than an intensifier alone.

AvoidWas it not for her courage, we would have failed.

Use insteadWere it not for her courage, we would have failed.

The inverted conditional with omitted “if” uses “Were,” not “Was,” regardless of subject.

Topic 9 of 10

Mastery of Register

Align grammar choices with academic, legal, journalistic, casual and technical styles at will.

  • Academic register favors nominalization, passive voice and careful hedging
  • Legal register favors precision, formal connectors and repeating nouns instead of pronouns
  • Casual register favors contractions, phrasal verbs and idioms
  • The same message can be recast for a specific genre without changing its meaning

AvoidThe defendant totally messed up, your honor.

Use insteadThe defendant acted negligently, your honor.

A legal register requires precise, formal vocabulary, not casual phrasing.

AvoidWe regret to inform you that we're bailing on the deal.

Use insteadWe regret to inform you that we are withdrawing from the agreement.

A formal opening should be matched by formal vocabulary throughout the sentence.

AvoidCan't make it 2 the meeting, sry.

Use insteadI am unable to attend the meeting; my apologies.

Text-speak abbreviations are inappropriate in a professional register.

Topic 10 of 10

Near-Native Transformation Skills

Rewrite entire passages while preserving meaning, and handle ambiguity and pragmatic nuance through grammar.

  • A good rewrite changes structure and word choice, not just individual synonyms
  • Passive/active choice affects who or what a sentence emphasizes
  • A dummy “it” subject is often needed when a clause is moved or nominalized
  • Stacking multiple connectors with the same function is redundant — choose one

AvoidRejected was the proposal by the board.

Use insteadThe board rejected the proposal.

Forcing an unnatural inversion doesn't make a rewrite more sophisticated — natural word order is usually clearer.

AvoidIs possible that he comes tomorrow.

Use insteadIt's possible that he'll come tomorrow.

English requires the dummy subject “it” before “is possible that.”

AvoidIn conclusion, to conclude, finally, the essay shows that…

Use insteadIn conclusion, the essay shows that…

Stacking several connectors with the same function is redundant; one is enough.
