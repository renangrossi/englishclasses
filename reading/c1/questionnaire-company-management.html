Talking About Your Own Work

The hardest thing to do well in a second language is explain what you actually do all day.

Most people who reach an advanced level in English can discuss politics, films and the weather, and then find themselves unable to explain their own job to somebody outside it. This is not a vocabulary problem. It is a structure problem, and it shows up in interviews, at conferences, and in the first ten minutes of every meeting with a new client.

The difficulty is that you know your work too well. You have stopped being able to see which parts of it are obvious and which are not, so you either start three layers too deep — naming systems and teams the listener has never heard of — or you retreat into something so general that it tells them nothing. “I work in technology” is true of several million people.

The reliable fix is to give a shape to the answer before you give the detail. A description of a role that works for a stranger has three parts, in this order: who you do it for, what problem you solve for them, and what you personally touch. “I work for a bank. When their customers move money between countries, something has to check it is not fraud before it goes through, and I look after the part that makes that decision.” Thirty seconds, no jargon, and the listener now has somewhere to put everything you say next.

The same shape works for a typical week, which is the question people answer worst. The bad answer is a list of meetings. The good one names the two or three things that actually consume the time and says what each is for. If you cannot say what a recurring meeting is for, that is worth knowing about your own week.

The question that separates a fluent speaker from a merely correct one is the difficult decision. Almost everybody tells it wrong, by giving the outcome first and the reasoning afterwards, which removes all the tension and makes the decision sound obvious. The order that works is the order you lived it: what the situation was, what made it genuinely hard — and if nothing made it hard, choose a different example — what you weighed against what, what you decided, and only then what happened. The last part matters least. A well-explained decision that turned out badly is far more impressive than a lucky one described in hindsight.

There is a matching trap in the question about challenges. The instinct is to name something external and unsolvable: the market, the budget, the pace of change. Those answers are safe and empty. The interesting ones are specific and admit a tension — that the people who understand the technology are rarely the people with the authority to fund it, say, or that every process you add to stop one kind of mistake makes the team slower at everything else.

Two smaller things. When you are asked how you keep up with your field, “I read a lot” is not an answer; name what, and say what you did because of something you read. And when you are asked what you would change about your industry, avoid the complaint everybody makes. The answer is more revealing than it looks, because what somebody chooses to be annoyed by tells you what they pay attention to.

Finally, the question that ends most of these conversations — is there anything else you would like to talk about? — is not politeness. It is the one moment where you choose the subject, and the only wrong answer is no.

Key Vocabulary

  • to show up to become visible or apparent
  • layer a level of detail
  • to retreat into to fall back on something safe when you are uncomfortable
  • jargon the specialist words of a trade, meaningless to outsiders
  • to look after to be responsible for something
  • to consume time to take up a large share of it
  • recurring happening again and again at regular intervals
  • to weigh one thing against another to compare the costs of two options before deciding
  • in hindsight looking back, knowing how it turned out
  • trap a mistake that is easy to walk into
  • instinct the first reaction you have without thinking
  • to keep up with to stay level with something that keeps moving
  • to be annoyed by to be irritated by something
  • to pay attention to to notice and take seriously

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Introduce your current role to somebody outside your industry, in thirty seconds, using the three-part shape: who you do it for, what problem you solve, what you personally touch.
  • What does a typical week look like for you? Name the two or three things that actually consume the time, and say what each is for.
  • Describe the most interesting thing you have worked on recently, and say precisely what made it interesting.
  • Describe a genuinely difficult decision in the order you lived it: the situation, what made it hard, what you weighed, what you chose, and only then what happened.
  • What is the biggest challenge in your field right now? Avoid the external and unsolvable answer — name something specific that contains a real tension.
  • How has the way you work changed in the last few years? Give a concrete example rather than a general trend.
  • How do you keep up with your field? Name what you actually read or attend, and one thing you did because of it.
  • What skills matter most in your work now that mattered less five years ago?
  • If you could change one thing about your industry, what would it be? Avoid the complaint everybody makes.
  • Which part of your work gives you the most satisfaction, and is that the same as the part you are best at?
  • Is there anything about your job you would like to talk about that nobody ever asks?
