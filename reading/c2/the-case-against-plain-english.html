The Case Against Plain English

Plain language is almost always right. This is an argument about the cases where it is not — and it concedes more than it wins.

ReadingPracticeDiscussion

Reading

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

Everyone is in favor of plain English, which should be the first warning. Positions that attract no opposition are rarely examined, and an unexamined position tends to be applied in places it was never meant to reach. The plain-language movement was right about almost everything it set out to fix, and the few things it got wrong are worth stating precisely, because stating them badly has given comfort to people who simply want to keep writing obscurely.

The case for plainness is overwhelming and should be conceded at once. Insurance contracts that no policyholder can parse, medical consent forms that defeat the patients signing them, benefit letters written in a register calculated to make a claimant give up — these are not neutral stylistic choices. They are transfers of power, carried out in prose, from the person who must act to the institution that drafted the document. Against that, plain English is not a matter of taste. It is closer to a civic obligation, and the movement that imposed it on government and finance did more practical good than most literary criticizm has managed in a century.

The first difficulty is that plainness is a property of the pairing of a text and its reader, not of the text alone. There is no such thing as a plain sentence in the abstract. A contract clause rewritten for a general audience may become genuinely unusable for the lawyers who have to litigate it, because the technical terms that made it forbidding were also what made it precise. Jargon has two distinct functions that look identical from outside: it excludes outsiders, and it lets insiders say exact things quickly. Strip it out for a specialist audience and you have not democratized the document, you have merely made it longer and vaguer while congratulating yourself.

The second difficulty is subtler, and it concerns what a sentence does to the reader's pace. Prose that is maximally easy to read is read quickly, and some things should not be read quickly. A sentence that requires you to hold two clauses in mind before it resolves has not failed; it has asked you to do something, and the asking is part of the meaning. Legal drafting that forces a slow re-reading at the exact point where a right is being waived is doing a job that a shorter, friendlier sentence would not do. Difficulty is not always a defect. It is sometimes a speed limit.

The third is a matter of trust, and here the evidence cuts both ways. Simplified official language has been shown to increase comprehension and, in some studies, to decrease the reader's sense that they are being told the whole truth — a document that sounds easy can sound as though something has been left out, particularly when the reader already has reason to be suspicious of the institution. That finding is not a defense of obscurity. It is a warning that plainness, mechanically applied, can cost credibility exactly where credibility is scarcest.

Now the concession, which is larger than everything above. In the overwhelming majority of real cases, the writer reaching for a complex sentence is not protecting precision, controlling pace or preserving trust. They are hedging, or padding, or signalling membership of a profession, or concealing that they have not decided what they think. The honest test is whether the difficulty is doing identifiable work, and the honest answer, nine times in ten, is that it is not. Anyone who takes the three arguments above as general permission has misread them, and will write worse than before with a clearer conscience.

So the position is narrow. Plain English should be the default, enforced by law where power is unequal, and departed from only where a writer can say precisely what the departure buys. That is a weaker claim than the title promised, and the weakening is the point: an argument that survives its own qualifications is worth more than one that was never tested against them.

What should be resisted is not plainness but the automatic reflex — the style guide applied without a reader in mind, the rule that every sentence be short, the assumption that the simpler of two drafts is the better one. The question is never whether a sentence is plain. It is what the sentence is for, and whether its difficulty, if it has any, is earning its keep.

Key Vocabulary

  • unexamined never tested or questioned
  • to concede to admit that a point against you is correct
  • to parse to work out the structure and meaning of
  • to defeat to be too difficult for somebody
  • claimant a person applying for a legal right or a benefit
  • to draft to write an official document
  • civic relating to the duties of a citizen
  • to litigate to argue a case in court
  • forbidding looking unfriendly or difficult to approach
  • jargon the specialist vocabulary of a trade or profession
  • to democratize to make available to everybody
  • to resolve to reach its completion or settlement
  • to waive to give up a right
  • defect a fault
  • to cut both ways to support each side of an argument equally
  • comprehension understanding
  • credibility the quality of being believed
  • scarce in short supply
  • concession a point you admit to the other side
  • to pad to add words that carry no content
  • to signal to show something indirectly, often about yourself
  • reflex an automatic reaction, done without thinking
  • to earn its keep to be worth what it costs

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Find a sentence in your own professional writing whose difficulty does no work. Rewrite it.
  • Is there a document in your field where plain language would genuinely cost precision?
  • The essay concedes most of its case. Does that make it more or less persuasive to you?
  • “Difficulty is sometimes a speed limit.” Where have you wanted a reader to slow down?
  • Write about 300 words arguing a position you believe, and include the strongest objection to it.
