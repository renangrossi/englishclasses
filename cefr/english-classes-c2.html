Aspect answers “how?”

  • Simple — a fact, a habit, a complete event
  • Continuous — a process, in the middle
  • Perfect — a result, looking back from a point
  • Perfect continuous — an activity and its duration

Tense answers “when?”

  • Present — now, or generally true
  • Past — finished time
  • Future — will / going to, seen from now

Tense as Style

Choosing tense and aspect as a rhetorical tool.

88

a) Habitual past in narrative

  • Every winter we would gather around the fire.
  • She used to hate coffee — now she lives on it.
would works for repeated actions only. For states (be, have, know, like), use used to or the simple past.

b) Continuous + always / constantly / forever

She always leaves her keys somewhere.a neutral fact
She’s always leaving her keys somewhere!irritation · a characteristic habit
He’s forever losing his phone.emotional color

c) Tense-shifting for effect

Historic presentSo I’m walking home, and suddenly this car stops right in front of me…
Fixed futureThe committee meets on Thursday. · The train departs in ten minutes.
Retrospective distanceLooking back, I had never really understood what he gave up for us.
Future perfect for certaintyBy thirty, she will have published three novels.

Sentence Architecture

Nesting clauses, fronting them — and keeping the reader on track.

89

a) Nested subordinate clauses

The proposal, which the board — most of whom had opposed it — reluctantly approved, outlined a plan that few believed would work.

main clause · relative clause · a clause inside the clause

b) Multi-clause fronting

  • Although the data seemed conclusive, and although several experts had endorsed it, the board remained unconvinced.
  • What had once seemed impossible, given the constraints, now looked achievable.
  • That she succeeded despite every obstacle is a testament to her resilience.

c) High-density noun phrases

  • The steady erosion of public trust in institutions once considered unimpeachable has reshaped political debate.
  • The gradual decline in voter turnout, a trend observed across most democracies, worries analysts.

Architectural principles

  • Use dashes or parentheses to insert a clause without breaking the flow.
  • Keep the main subject and verb easy to find, even in a long sentence.
  • Balance: a very long sentence needs a short one nearby.

Discourse Devices

Guiding a reader through an argument, beyond “however”.

90

a) Formal connectors

notwithstanding= despiteThe plan, criticism notwithstanding, went ahead.
nevertheless · nonetheless= despite thatThe evidence was weak. Nonetheless, he was convicted.
moreover · furthermore= a stronger pointIt’s costly. Moreover, it doesn’t work.
to this end= for this purposeWe need data. To this end, a survey was launched.

b) Structuring markers

that saidacknowledge, then pivotThat said, not every critic dismissed it.
insofar asto the extent thatIt worked insofar as it cut emissions.
by the same tokensame logic, related pointBy the same token, we must accept its costs.
converselyparallel contrastConversely, small firms gained.

c) Cohesion across paragraphs

  • This trend · such measures · this development · the aforementioned issue
  • It is worth noting that… · This raises the question of… · Having established X, it follows that…

Stylistic Transformations

One idea, many grammatical forms — shifting tone and register.

91

a) Active or passive — on purpose

The board rejected the proposal.→The proposal was rejected, a decision that surprised many. · agent removed

b) Same content, different tone

BluntThe plan failed.
Hedged · academicIt could be argued that the plan did not achieve its intended outcomes.
Formal · officialThe initiative did not meet its stated objectives.
RhetoricalCan anyone really claim that this plan succeeded?

Informal → formal

They messed up the schedule badly.→The scheduling was severely mismanaged.
We’re cutting costs next quarter.→Cost reductions will be implemented next quarter.
Nobody knows why it failed.→The precise cause of the failure remains unclear.
Fix this now!→This issue requires immediate attention.

c) Grammar that signals register

nominalization + passiveformal · academic · legal
modal hedging (may, might, it would seem)cautious · academic
contractions · phrasal verbs · imperativesinformal · conversational
clefts · frontingemphatic · persuasive

Ellipsis & Substitution

Leave out what’s obvious; replace what’s repeated.

92

a) Ellipsis — omit recoverable words

after and / but / orShe ordered coffee and she ordered a croissant.
in comparisonsHe works harder than she works. · Some like tea; others like coffee.
after auxiliariesHe hasn’t finished, but he will finish soon.
same-subject clausesWhile he was waiting, he read the paper.

b) Omission in formal writing

  • The book that I mentioned is out.
  • The results, which were published last year, remain controversial.

c) Pro-forms

do soHe asked her to leave, and she did so at once.
oneNeed a pen? Take one from my desk.
the sameThe first study confirmed it; the second did the same.
so + aux + subjectShe speaks French, and so does her sister.

Literary Clefts & Inversions

Rhetorical and descriptive word order for essays, speeches and prose.

93

a) Literary inversion

place / directionDown came the rain. · Deep beneath the city lie ancient tunnels.
participleGone are the days of handwritten letters.
so + adjectiveSo convincing was her argument that the panel changed its mind.
suchSuch was the outcry that the company apologized.
comparisonEqually significant is the role of education.

b) Layered pseudo-clefts

What matters is not what you achieve, but what you learn. What most people fail to see is that failure itself is what teaches the most.

c) Fronted modifiers

  • Exhausted but triumphant, the team crossed the line.
  • Exhausted yet undeterred, the climbers pressed on.
Deliberately literary — powerful in writing and speeches, theatrical in casual talk.

Modality & Attitude

Tentativeness, concession, politeness and stance.

94

a) Subtle shading

HedgingIt would seem that the talks have stalled.
Concession: may / might + wellShe may well be right, but I have doubts.
Politeness distanceMight I suggest we revisit this later?

b) Layered modality

  • He might have been expected to resign, yet he stayed.
  • She should, in theory, have been told beforehand.
  • They were supposed to have finished by now.

c) Stance markers — how sure am I?

It stands to reason that…
confident
There is reason to believe that…
fairly sure
It is conceivable that…
possible
One cannot rule out that…
cautious

Rare & Literary Structures

Subjunctive, emphatic “do” and archaic inversion.

95

a) The subjunctive — base form, no -s

  • The committee demanded that he resign.
  • It is essential that every member be present.
  • The board recommended that the policy be reviewed.
So be it.Suffice it to say…Be that as it may…Come what may…

b) Emphatic do

  • She did warn us — nobody listened.
  • I do understand your concern.
  • This decision did shape the rest of my life.

c) Archaic inverted conditionals

  • Had I but known, I would have acted differently.
  • Were it not for her help, the deal would have collapsed.
  • Be it ever so humble, there’s no place like home.
Not needed for everyday fluency — but essential for literature, legal texts and ceremonies, and powerful used sparingly in writing.

Register

The same content, recast for five contexts.

96

One fact · five registers

RegisterFeaturesExample
Academicnominalization · passive · hedgingThe data suggest a strong correlation between sleep and performance.
Legalshall · pursuant to · exact repetitionThe Tenant shall provide written notice no fewer than thirty (30) days prior to termination.
Journalisticfront-loaded · attributed · activeThe bridge will remain closed, officials confirmed on Tuesday.
Casualcontractions · phrasal verbs · ellipsisSo they’re closing the bridge — no idea for how long, though.
Technicalimperatives · precise terms · no hedgingDisconnect the device before servicing. Do not exceed the rated voltage.

What to adjust

  • sentence length and complexity
  • active or passive voice
  • vocabulary formality
  • contractions and pronouns
  • how claims are attributed or hedged

Transformation Skills

Rewrite whole passages — and control ambiguity and tone.

97

a) Rewrite the passage, keep the meaning

Original

The company grew quickly. It hired hundreds of people. This caused training problems.

Rewritten

As the company expanded rapidly, hiring hundreds of new staff in a short period, significant training problems inevitably followed.

b) Ambiguity — keep it or remove it

Deliberately vagueMistakes were made. (no agent — no blame)
PreciseThe finance team made several errors in the Q3 report.
AmbiguousVisiting relatives can be tiring.
ClarifiedRelatives who come to visit can be tiring. / Going to visit relatives…

c) Pragmatic nuance

You made a mistake in the report.→There may have been a small oversight in the report — perhaps worth a second look. · softened
The plan was badly designed.→It is not that the plan was poorly designed; rather, circumstances changed. · reframed
Near-native control means the reader feels the tone you intend — without noticing the grammar that creates it.
