Japan's Most Famous Dog

A bronze dog outside Shibuya station, and the nine years that put him there.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

In front of Shibuya station in Tokyo, one of the busiest railway stations in the world, there is a life-size bronze statue of a dog. It is small next to the enormous screens and neon signs above it, and it is still the easiest thing in the area to find, because there is always a crowd standing around it. Since the 1930s it has been the place people in Tokyo arrange to meet. You do not look for the statue. You look for the people waiting.

The dog was called Hachiko. He was an Akita, born in 1923 and brought to Tokyo the following year by Professor Ueno, who taught agricultural science at what was then the Imperial University. The two of them were inseparable almost immediately. Every morning Hachiko walked with him to the station and watched him leave for work, and every evening he was there again when the professor came back.

In 1925, a little over a year after Hachiko arrived, the professor had a brain haemorrhage at the university and died. He did not come home, and he did not come back to the station.

Hachiko kept going. He returned to Shibuya at roughly the same hour, day after day, and waited. He did this for nine years, until his own death in 1935.

It is worth being careful about the story, because it has been retold so many times that the sentimental version has almost replaced the facts. Hachiko did not live at the station the whole time; he was taken in by former gardeners and relatives of the professor, and he came to the station from where he lived. Street vendors and commuters fed him, and some people have argued that the food is the real reason he kept coming back — an argument that is impossible to settle and, to be fair, does not explain the timing, since he tended to arrive around the hour the train had once brought the professor in.

What is certain is that he was there for nine years, that the commuters came to know him, and that a newspaper article in 1932 made him famous across the country. The statue went up in 1934, while he was still alive, and he is said to have attended the unveiling. The original was melted down for metal during the war; the one standing there now was made in 1948 by the son of the sculptor who made the first.

Hachiko died in March 1935, near the station, of causes that were only established much later — he had cancer and a heart infection, and, as it turned out, several skewers from grilled meat in his stomach, which had not killed him.

The story is told in Japan as a lesson about loyalty, and abroad, usually, as a sad film. What is genuinely striking about it is smaller than either: an animal built a habit around a person, the person stopped arriving, and the habit held for nine years in a place where several hundred thousand strangers walked past it every day.

Key Vocabulary

  • life-size the same size as the real thing
  • bronze a hard brown metal used for statues
  • to arrange to meet to agree a place and time to see somebody
  • inseparable always together; impossible to keep apart
  • haemorrhage heavy bleeding, here inside the brain
  • sentimental appealing to feeling more than to fact
  • to take someone in to give somebody a home
  • vendor somebody who sells things, often in the street
  • commuter somebody who travels to work every day
  • to settle an argument to end it by showing which side is right
  • to be fair a phrase used when you admit a point against your own case
  • unveiling the ceremony where a statue is shown to the public for the first time
  • to melt down to heat metal until it becomes liquid, so it can be reused
  • skewer a thin stick used to hold food while it is grilled
  • loyalty staying faithful to somebody, even when there is nothing to gain
  • striking noticeable and hard to forget
  • a stranger a person you do not know — careful: not a person from another country

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The writer separates the loyalty story from what can actually be established. Does the skeptical version make the story less moving?
  • “He is said to have attended.” Why does a careful writer use that phrasing instead of simply saying he was there?
  • The statue is used as a meeting point by people who may not know the story at all. Does that matter?
  • Is there a famous animal story in your country? How much of it do you think is true?
  • Write about 150 words about a habit somebody you know has kept for years, and say what started it.
