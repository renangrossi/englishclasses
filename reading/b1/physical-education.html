The PE Teacher

Five years of getting children to move, and what she has learned about how to do it.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

Caroline has taught physical education at the same primary school for five years, and in that time she has worked out something that took her a while to accept: most children do not dislike exercise. They dislike being bad at it in front of other people. Once she understood that, she changed how she taught.

The first thing she did was stop picking teams the old way. Standing two children at the front to choose their friends in order tells every child exactly where they rank, and the ones picked last had usually stopped trying before the game began. Now she divides the class herself, differently every week, and nobody knows who is supposed to be the good one.

She has introduced a lot of games over the years — tag, small-sided soccer, relays with teams too short to hide in. The equipment changed too: hoops and balls of different sizes, because a child who cannot catch a tennis ball can catch a beach ball and will keep trying. Music turned out to work far better than she expected, and several children who refused to run will dance for twenty minutes without noticing.

Last month she ran a relay race that the whole school still talks about. She set it up so that each team needed a fast runner, a careful one and somebody who could throw, which meant no team could win on speed alone. Three children who had never been picked for anything ended up deciding their races. She has run it twice since.

Not everything has worked. She tried yoga two years ago, hoping it would help the children who arrive at school already anxious, and the first attempt was a failure because she scheduled it straight after lunch when they were at their loudest. She moved it to the end of the day and it has been one of her most popular sessions ever since. She has kept the lesson and thrown away the timetable, which is the sort of adjustment nobody tells you about before you start teaching.

Next week she is running a treasure hunt across the playground. The clues are already hidden, and she has written them so that each one needs two children to solve it — one has to read and one has to count. In the spring she is planning a swimming day, if the weather is warm enough, and she wants to start a lunchtime fitness club before the end of the year. Parents are being invited to the sports afternoons now, which some of them find more intimidating than the children do.

Ask her what the job is actually about and she will not say fitness. She says her aim is that a child leaves her class at eleven still willing to try something they are not yet good at. Everything else, she says, follows from that.

Key Vocabulary

  • primary school a school for children roughly aged five to eleven
  • to work something out to come to understand something after thinking about it
  • to rank to place in order of ability
  • small-sided played with fewer players per team than the full game
  • relay a race in which team members take turns to run
  • hoop a large plastic ring used in games and exercise
  • to set something up to arrange or organize it in advance
  • to schedule to fix the time at which something happens
  • timetable the plan of when each lesson happens
  • clue a piece of information that helps solve a puzzle
  • intimidating making someone feel nervous or inadequate
  • aim what you are trying to achieve

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Caroline says children dislike being bad at something in public, not the activity itself. Is that true of adults as well?
  • Were teams picked in front of you at school? What did it feel like, and what would you have changed?
  • She designed a race that could not be won on speed alone. Is that fair to the fastest children?
  • “She kept the lesson and threw away the timetable.” Describe something that failed for you because of when, not what.
  • Write about 120 words about a teacher who changed how you felt about a subject, using the present perfect for what they have done and the past simple for one specific memory.
