A2 Review: The Four Tenses

Present simple, present continuous, past simple and the future — what each one is actually for.

Most students at this level already know how to build the four main tenses. What is harder is knowing which one a sentence needs, and that is usually decided by the time word, not by the verb.

The present simple is for things that are generally true, and for habits. I work in a hospital. She goes to bed at half past ten. We do not like coffee. The words that go with it are always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day, on Saturdays. If you can put “every week” into the sentence without it sounding strange, you probably want the present simple.

The present continuous is for right now, or for a period around now. Be quiet — the baby is sleeping. Look! It is raining. I am reading a very long book at the moment. Its words are now, right now, at the moment, today, and the imperatives look and listen, which almost always introduce it. Notice the difference: “He works in a hospital” is his job, and “He is working on Saturday” is this weekend only.

The past simple is for finished actions at a finished time. We went to the lake last Saturday. She did not come to the party. The time word — yesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago — is what forces it, and once that word is in the sentence no other tense is possible. The difficulty is not the rule but the verbs: about half of the common ones are irregular and simply have to be learned. Went, saw, took, ran, swam, caught, bought, brought.

For the future, English gives you three options and they are not interchangeable. Use will for a decision made as you speak, or a prediction: I will help you with that. It will rain tomorrow. Use going to for an intention you already had: I am going to look for a new job. And use the present continuous for something already arranged, usually with another person: I am meeting Sarah at six. The last one surprises learners, because it looks like the present.

One last thing that causes trouble. Some verbs do not normally take the continuous, even when you are talking about this moment: know, want, like, need, understand, believe. You say I know the answer, never I am knowing the answer, and this is one of the few places where English is stricter than most languages.

Vocabulary

  • to build — to put a sentence or a form together
  • habit — something you do regularly, usually without thinking
  • time word — a word such as yesterday or always that shows when something happens
  • to sound strange — to feel wrong to a native speaker, even if the grammar is correct
  • imperative — a command form such as look or listen
  • to force — to leave no other choice
  • irregular — not following the usual rule
  • interchangeable — able to be used in place of one another
  • to arrange — to fix something in advance with somebody else
  • strict — allowing no exceptions

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • Say three things you do every week, and three things you are doing this week.
  • What did you do last weekend? Use five past simple verbs, and try to include two irregular ones.
  • Say one thing you will do, one you are going to do, and one you are doing — and explain why each takes that form.
  • Which of the four tenses is hardest for you? What goes wrong?
  • Write about 100 words about your week: what you usually do, what you are doing now, and what you will do next.
