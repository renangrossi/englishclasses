How a Formula One Car Works

A hybrid power unit, a wing that pushes down instead of up, and a car built to be light in the places that matter.

A Formula One car is a single-seater built to do two things an ordinary car cannot: accelerate extremely hard in a straight line, and change direction at speeds where a road car would simply leave the track. Almost every design decision follows from the second of those.

The engine is a hybrid power unit, and the word is accurate rather than marketing. It combines a small turbocharged internal combustion engine — 1.6 litres, which is less than most family cars — with an energy recovery system. The combustion engine converts fuel into the power that drives the rear wheels. The recovery system captures energy that would otherwise be thrown away as heat under braking, stores it, and returns it as a boost the driver can deploy. The combined output is over a thousand horsepower from an engine the size of a suitcase, and the thermal efficiency of these units is higher than any road engine ever built.

Aerodynamics is where the real difference lies, and the principle is counter-intuitive. An aircraft wing is shaped to produce lift. An F1 wing is the same shape inverted, so it produces the opposite: downforce, pressing the car onto the track. The faster the car goes, the harder it is pressed down, and the more grip the tyres have. This is why these cars corner at speeds that seem impossible — they are not relying on the weight of the car but on air pushing it into the ground. At full speed the downforce exceeds the weight of the car several times over, which is the origin of the often-repeated claim that the car could in theory drive along a ceiling.

The consequence is that grip depends on speed, which makes the car behave in a way that takes drivers a long time to trust. Slow corners have relatively little downforce and the car slides; fast corners have enormous downforce and the car holds. The instinct to brake harder in a fast corner is exactly wrong, because slowing down is what removes the grip.

Suspension keeps the tyres in contact with an uneven surface and, just as importantly, holds the car at the right height and angle for the aerodynamics to work. It is extremely stiff — far stiffer than anything road-legal — because a car that moves on its springs is a car whose wings are constantly at the wrong angle.

The structure is built from carbon fibre composites, which give a lightweight shell with more strength for its mass than steel. The minimum weight is set by the rules, so teams do not simply build the lightest car possible; they build a car that is under the limit and then add ballast exactly where they want it, which is a way of controlling balance that is not available if the car is already too heavy.

Finally, the car reports on itself constantly. A modern car carries several hundred sensors feeding telemetry to the pit wall in real time — tyre temperatures, brake temperatures, fuel flow, pressures, the loads going through the suspension. The engineers often know what has failed before the driver has finished the lap, and the strategy decisions that decide most races are made from that data rather than from what anybody can see.

Vocabulary

  • single-seater — a racing car with room for the driver only
  • hybrid — using two power sources together, here petrol and recovered electrical energy
  • turbocharged — using exhaust gas to force extra air into the engine for more power
  • internal combustion engine — an engine that burns fuel inside its own cylinders
  • energy recovery system — equipment that captures energy normally lost and stores it for reuse
  • to deploy — to release or use something at a chosen moment
  • thermal efficiency — how much of a fuel's energy an engine turns into useful work
  • aerodynamics — the study of how air flows around a moving object
  • counter-intuitive — true, but the opposite of what you would expect
  • lift — the upward force a wing generates
  • inverted — turned upside down
  • downforce — aerodynamic force pressing a car onto the track
  • grip — the hold the tyres keep on the surface
  • to exceed — to be greater than
  • stiff — resisting bending or movement
  • carbon fibre composite — a material of carbon threads set in resin, very strong for its weight
  • lightweight — weighing little for its size
  • ballast — weight added deliberately to reach a limit or adjust balance
  • telemetry — measurements transmitted automatically from a machine to those monitoring it
  • pit wall — where the team's engineers sit and direct the race

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • The car's grip depends on its speed, which means the safe reaction feels wrong. Where else does correct action feel counter-intuitive?
  • Engineers often know a component has failed before the driver does. Does that make the driver's judgement less important?
  • Formula One develops technology that later reaches road cars. Is that a good justification for the sport's cost?
  • The minimum weight is set by rules, so teams compete on where to put the weight rather than how little to have. What does that say about how regulations shape engineering?
  • Write about 150 words explaining a piece of technology from a sport you follow, to someone who does not follow it.
