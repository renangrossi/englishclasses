Protecting Your Own Accounts

Five things that actually work, and why the advice you were given ten years ago was wrong.

Most people's picture of online crime involves somebody breaking through a wall of security. Almost none of it works that way. The accounts that get taken over are taken over because a password was reused, or because somebody was persuaded to type it into the wrong box, and the defences that help are correspondingly unglamorous.

The first is not reusing passwords. This matters more than making them complicated. When a company is breached, the stolen list of email addresses and passwords is sold, and the buyers try those combinations on every other service they can think of. If your email password is also your bank password, one company's bad day becomes yours. A password used in exactly one place is worth more than a clever password used in five.

The advice to build passwords out of substituted characters — replacing an o with a zero, adding an exclamation mark at the end — has quietly been abandoned, because the software that guesses passwords has known those tricks for twenty years, and because it produces passwords that are hard for people to remember and easy for machines to crack. A long ordinary phrase is stronger than a short mangled word. The practical answer for most people is a password manager: one genuinely strong password you remember, and a program that stores the rest, so that they can all be long and all be different without you memorising anything.

The second defence is two-factor authentication, usually a code from an app on your phone in addition to your password. It is the single most effective thing on this list, because it means a stolen password on its own is not enough. It is also mildly annoying, which is why many people do not switch it on, and that trade — a few seconds against losing an account entirely — is the whole argument.

The third is installing updates. A great many attacks use a weakness that was found, reported, and fixed months earlier, and they work only on devices where the fix was never installed. Postponing an update for a week is fine. Postponing it for a year is how an ordinary phone becomes an easy target.

The fourth is suspicion about messages. Phishing means a message designed to make you hand over a password or open an attachment, and the good ones do not look suspicious at all — they look like your bank, or a delivery company, or your employer's IT department. What they share is urgency: your account will be closed, a payment has failed, act now. Urgency is the tell. A genuine organisation is content for you to close the message, open the app yourself, and check.

The fifth is backups, which are not about crime until suddenly they are. If a device is lost, broken or locked by ransomware, the only thing that matters is whether a copy of your files exists somewhere else. The rule people use is three copies, on two kinds of storage, with one somewhere other than your home.

None of this makes anybody safe, which is not the standard to aim at. The standard is being harder work than the next person, because almost all of this is automated and automated attacks move on. Most of the damage in ordinary people's lives comes from a small number of very common mistakes, and five habits remove nearly all of them.

Vocabulary

  • to take over — to gain control of something that belongs to someone else
  • to reuse — to use the same thing again in another place
  • unglamorous — dull and unimpressive, though effective
  • to breach — to break into a system and take data from it
  • combination — a particular pairing, such as an email address and a password
  • to substitute — to put one thing in place of another
  • to abandon — to stop using or believing something
  • to crack — to work out a password or code by force
  • mangled — damaged and distorted out of its proper form
  • password manager — a program that stores all your passwords behind one master password
  • two-factor authentication — proving who you are with two separate things, such as a password and a phone code
  • trade — an exchange in which you accept one cost to avoid another
  • to postpone — to delay something to a later time
  • phishing — a message designed to trick you into giving up a password or opening a harmful file
  • attachment — a file sent together with a message
  • urgency — pressure to act immediately
  • tell — a small sign that gives away what is really going on
  • ransomware — software that locks your files and demands payment to release them
  • backup — a spare copy of your files kept in case the original is lost
  • automated — carried out by software without a person directing each step

Speaking & Writing

Discussion

There are no right answers here — use these to practice speaking, or write a short answer to each.

  • “Urgency is the tell.” Think of the last message that tried to hurry you. What was it asking for?
  • Two-factor authentication is effective and annoying. Where else do you accept a small daily cost to avoid a rare large one?
  • The advice about substituted characters was taught for years and is now abandoned. How should ordinary people keep up when expert advice changes?
  • Is “being harder work than the next person” a fair standard, or does it just move the problem to someone else?
  • Write about 150 words giving security advice to a relative who is not confident with technology. Choose the two things you would tell them first.
