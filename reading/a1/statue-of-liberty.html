The Statue of Liberty

A present from France, a long trip in 214 boxes, and the first thing new Americans saw.

Play the audio and follow along, then read it again on your own. Tap or hover over a highlighted word to see what it means.

In New York, a green woman stands in the water. She is forty-six meters tall. In her right hand she has a light.

The statue is a present. It is a present from France to the United States.

A French artist, Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, made it. People in France paid for the statue. People in the United States paid for the big stone under it.

In 1885 the statue came to New York in a ship, in 214 big boxes. Workers built it on a small island. It opened in October 1886.

The statue is green now, but it was brown at first. It is made of copper, and copper changes color in the rain and the wind. This takes about thirty years.

After 1892, ships with new people from Europe arrived next to the island. The statue was the first big thing they saw in America.

Many of these people were poor. They were tired after two or three weeks on the sea. Some of them cried.

There are words on the statue, from a poem by Emma Lazarus: “Give me your tired, your poor...”

Today you can take a boat to the island. You can go inside the statue and look at New York from her head.

More than four million people visit her every year. She is not only a statue now. For many people, she is a word: welcome.

Key Vocabulary

  • a statue a figure of a person made of stone or metal
  • a present something you give to somebody
  • copper a brown metal that turns green in the air and the rain
  • an island land with water all around it
  • to build to make something big, like a house (past: built)
  • a poem a short piece of writing with special rhythm
  • to arrive to come to a place
  • to visit to go and see a place or a person
  • welcome a happy word for a person who arrives
